# Independent audit: determinant ranges over rings

This note concerns diagonal matrices and unrestricted invertible similarity.
Its coefficient identity is not a positive convex representation over the
complex numbers. No claim of a solution of complex MOC is made.

## 1. The statement valid over every commutative ring

Let R be a commutative ring with identity, A=diag(a_1,...,a_n),
B=diag(b_1,...,b_n), and U in GL_n(R). Set

    z_pi = product_j (a_pi(j)+b_j),
    d(U) = det(A+UBU^(-1)).

Because (A+UBU^(-1))U=AU+UB, determinant expansion gives the exact identity

    d(U) = sum_pi c_pi z_pi,
    c_pi = sgn(pi) product_j U_pi(j),j / det(U),
    sum_pi c_pi = 1.                                      (1)

Division by det(U) is legitimate because it is a unit of R. If z_0 is any
one vertex and I=(z_pi-z_0 : pi in S_n), then

    d(U) in z_0+I.                                       (2)

This is an affine-ideal containment, with no assertion about signs.

The GL and SL ranges are identical: multiply U on the right by
diag(det(U)^(-1),1,...,1). This diagonal factor commutes with B, preserves
the conjugate UBU^(-1), and corrects the determinant to one.

## 2. A constructive sufficient condition for equality

Two pairing vertices are adjacent when their pairings differ by exchanging
two assigned rows. Their differences generate I: the transposition graph
of S_n is connected, so every vertex difference is a sum of edge differences.

For an adjacent pair z_pi,z_tau, insert the block

    G(t) = [[1,1],[t,1+t]],       det(G(t))=1,

on the corresponding row coordinates, and multiply the appropriate
permutation matrix on the left by G(t). Its only two nonzero permutation
terms in (1) have coefficients 1+t and -t. Therefore it realizes

    d(U(t)) = (1+t) z_pi - t z_tau
            = z_pi+t(z_pi-z_tau),       t in R.           (3)

If one transposition difference generates I, equation (3) proves that the
range is exactly z_0+I. In particular:

* This equality holds for n=2 over every commutative ring.
* It holds over every field: the range is a singleton if all vertices agree,
  and otherwise the whole field.
* It holds over every chain ring (a ring whose ideals are linearly ordered),
  because one of the finitely many edge differences generates their ideal.

The last point includes valuation rings, finite quotients of discrete
valuation rings, and the local cases used in the p-adic argument.

## 3. Integer spectra: divisibility and every compatible congruence

For integer spectra define the nonnegative integer

    g = gcd{z_pi-z_0 : pi in S_n}.

Every U in GL_n(Z) satisfies d(U)-z_0 in gZ. If g=0, the range is the
singleton z_0. If g is nonzero, this is a divisibility restriction.

There is also an exact, stronger congruence statement. For every positive
integer M and every integer h satisfying

    h-z_0 in gZ + MZ,

there exists U in SL_n(Z) with d(U)=h modulo M.

To prove this, work first modulo each prime power p^k dividing M. Among
the finitely many transposition differences, one has minimal p-adic
valuation. Its reduction generates the ideal of all vertex differences
in Z/(p^k), so (3) realizes every compatible residue there. The Chinese
remainder theorem patches these local matrices to a matrix in SL_n(Z/MZ).
For n>=2 this matrix lifts to SL_n(Z): SL_n over a finite local ring is
generated by elementary matrices (a unit pivot exists in a unimodular
column, and elementary row operations reduce the matrix); Chinese
remaindering gives the same generation over Z/MZ; then lift the elementary
coefficients to integers. Dimension one is immediate.

Consequently the integer determinant values are profinitely dense in
z_0+gZ. Finite-field searches and searches modulo prime powers cannot
detect any additional obstruction. This does NOT establish that every
integer in that coset is attained as an exact integer determinant.

The analogous local congruence argument works in Z[i], using its prime-power
quotients and elementary matrix lifting. No unrestricted exact PID range
theorem is asserted here.

## 4. An exact integer example beyond the single-edge criterion

Take A=diag(0,1,3), B=diag(0,2,5). In lexicographic permutation order the
vertices are

    0, 0, 16, 25, 36, 45.

Their difference gcd is one, but no transposition-edge difference is 1 or
-1. Nevertheless every integer N is attained. Explicitly,

    U(N) = [[1,       1,       1],
            [5N,      5N+1,    5N+1],
            [-9N,     -9N,     1-9N]].

Direct polynomial determinant expansion gives

    det(U(N)) = 1,
    det(A U(N)+U(N) B) = N.

Thus d(U(N))=N. The accompanying standard-library checker verifies both
identities as polynomial identities with integer coefficients. This is an
example, not an argument for arbitrary integer spectra.


## Scope of the audit

The ring identity, interpolation, congruence theorem, explicit integral
example, and polynomial-ring obstruction are self-contained. They concern
similarity with U^(-1), not the complex unitary-conjugacy convex-hull
conjecture. An algebraic affine combination over a ring cannot be promoted
to positive real convex weights without an independent argument.
