## Every complex four-dimensional minor profile is a mixture of real profiles

### The theorem

For every \(U\in U(4)\), there are \(s\le4\), matrices \(O_1,\ldots,O_s\in SO(4)\), and probabilities \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_s\) such that

\[
|\det U[I,J]|^2
=\sum_{k=1}^s\lambda_k|\det O_k[I,J]|^2
\tag{2}
\]

for **every** pair of equally sized row and column subsets. Consequently,

\[
\det(D_a+UD_bU^*)
=\sum_{k=1}^s\lambda_k\det(D_a+O_kD_bO_k^T)
\tag{3}
\]

for all \(a,b\in\mathbb C^4\), with the same matrices and probabilities.

Thus MOC in dimension four is equivalent to its restriction to real orthogonal relative eigenbases. The spectra in that restricted problem remain arbitrary complex numbers. Equation (2) is a law on orthogonal matrices; it is not the stronger, false assertion of a common law on permutations.

### A real three-effect decomposition

Let \(E_1,E_2,E_3\) be real symmetric positive semidefinite operators on a three-dimensional real space, with

\[
\operatorname{tr}E_i=1,\qquad E_1+E_2+E_3=I.
\]

Every such triple is a convex mixture of triples of orthogonal rank-one projections.

To prove this, consider an extreme point of the compact convex set of such triples. If all three ranks are at least two, symmetric perturbations supported on the three ranges have a total of at least \(3+3+3=9\) dimensions. The sum-zero condition gives six scalar equations. The three trace-zero conditions give at most two further independent equations, since their sum already follows from the sum-zero equation. A nonzero supported perturbation therefore exists. Small perturbations of either sign preserve positivity and all constraints, contradicting extremality.

An extreme triple must therefore have a rank-one effect. Trace one makes it a projection \(P\). The other two effects are supported on \(P^\perp\), where they sum to the two-dimensional identity. They commute. Their eigenvalues are \(\theta,1-\theta\) and \(1-\theta,\theta\), so the triple is a mixture of the two orthonormal bases obtained by exchanging those complementary vectors. At an extreme point this mixture must be trivial. Compact convexity proves the assertion.

For the effects used in (2), each rank is at most two. If one already has rank one, two bases suffice. Otherwise all ranks are two. Move along any nonzero supported perturbation to the two endpoints of its maximal positive interval. At each endpoint some rank drops to one, because traces remain one and the feasible set is bounded. Each endpoint requires at most two bases. Mixing the two endpoints gives **at most four bases**.

Oszmaniec, Guerini, Wittek and Acín prove the corresponding complex three-dimensional trace-one effect decomposition in Lemma 7 of their 2017 paper. The real argument, the four-term refinement for these particular effects, and the minor application have been supplied explicitly here. See [the primary paper](https://arxiv.org/html/1609.06139v2).

### Apply the lemma to a unitary matrix

Choose row 2. Multiplying columns by phases makes that row real and nonnegative:

\[
v=(|U_{21}|,\ldots,|U_{24}|),\qquad \|v\|=1.
\]

This gauge changes neither squared minors nor \(UD_bU^*\). Write each of the other rows, transposed, as a complex column \(x_i\), and put

\[
E_i=\operatorname{Re}(x_ix_i^*),\qquad i=1,3,4.
\]

For real \(y\), \(y^TE_iy=|x_i^*y|^2\ge0\). Each effect has trace one, rank at most two, and annihilates \(v\). Unitarity gives

\[
E_1+E_3+E_4=I-vv^T.
\]

On the real three-space \(v^\perp\), the four-term lemma applies. Insert \(v\) as the fixed second row of every resulting real orthonormal basis. This constructs the matrices \(O_k\).

Singleton probabilities are the diagonal entries of the \(E_i\), so they are preserved. Every two-minor containing the fixed row has the form

\[
|x_{ij}v_\ell-x_{i\ell}v_j|^2
=(v_\ell e_j-v_je_\ell)^TE_i(v_\ell e_j-v_je_\ell),
\]

which is linear in \(E_i\), and is also preserved. A two-row subset omitting row 2 has a two-row complement containing row 2. Complementary-minor identities for unitary matrices therefore preserve the other two-minors. Three-minors are complementary to singletons; empty and full minor moduli equal one. This proves (2). Row sign changes in rows other than the fixed row make every determinant \(+1\), if necessary.

Finally, expansion in the diagonal entries of \(D_a\), followed by Cauchy–Binet, gives

\[
d(U)=\sum_{|I|=|J|}a_{I^c}b_J|\det U[I,J]|^2.
\]

Substitution of (2) proves (3).

### Why this dimension reduction stops at a genuine boundary

In dimension five, the same construction leaves four effects on a real four-space. There exist extreme trace-one four-effect tuples of rank two that do not decompose into orthogonal projection frames. An exact rational example generated by a complex orthonormal basis is saved in `algebra_real_povm4_obstruction.json`.

It gives an actual obstruction to **fixed-row** five-dimensional realification. Set

\[
v=(-3,1,1,1,2)/4,\quad
S=I-ss^T/28,\quad s=(7,-1,-1,-1,-2).
\]

Then \(S\) is a rational Householder matrix whose first row is \(v\). Let \(W\in U(4)\) be the exact basis from the certificate and take \(U=\operatorname{diag}(1,W)S\). If a real-profile mixture with that same row \(v\) existed, singleton and anchored two-minor data would recover every effect entry, because all \(v_jv_k\ne0\):

\[
(E_i)_{jk}
=\frac{v_k^2(E_i)_{jj}+v_j^2(E_i)_{kk}
-|U_{ij}v_k-U_{ik}v_j|^2}{2v_jv_k}.
\]

The mixture would decompose the extreme nonprojective effect tuple into frames, a contradiction. This obstructs the fixed-row method. It does not disprove unrestricted convexification from \(U(5)\) to \(O(5)\), or MOC in dimension five.

## A common rank-two compression law with three parents on one side

### The theorem and its coefficients

Let \(X=[x_1,x_2,x_3]\in\mathbb C^{2\times3}\) and \(Y=[y_1,\ldots,y_n]\in\mathbb C^{2\times n}\) be coisometries:

\[
XX^*=YY^*=I_2.
\]

There is one probability law, independent of \(a\in\mathbb C^3,b\in\mathbb C^n\), proving

\[
\det(XD_aX^*+YD_bY^*)
\in\operatorname{conv}\{(a_i+b_j)(a_k+b_\ell):i\ne k,\ j\ne\ell\}.
\tag{4}
\]

Define

\[
q^X_{ik}=|\det[x_i,x_k]|^2,\quad
q^Y_{j\ell}=|\det[y_j,y_\ell]|^2,\quad
F_{ij}=|\det[x_i,y_j]|^2.
\]

The law simultaneously realizes the selected left-pair probabilities \(q^X\), selected right-pair probabilities \(q^Y\), and mixed incidences \(F\). The proof works for every finite \(n\), without finite-dimensional enumeration.

### An exact colored-edge allocation theorem

Give edges \(\{j,\ell\}\) of a finite graph nonnegative masses \(q_{j\ell}\) summing to one. Put \(d_j=\sum_{\ell\ne j}q_{j\ell}\). For a vertex subset \(S\), let \(\operatorname{inc}_q(S)\) be the mass of edges meeting \(S\).

Suppose \(F_{ij}\ge0\) and \(\sum_iF_{ij}=d_j\). We want to assign different endpoint colors to each edge, preserving its mass, so that color \(i\) occurs at vertex \(j\) with total mass \(F_{ij}\). Such an allocation exists exactly when

\[
F_i(S):=\sum_{j\in S}F_{ij}\le\operatorname{inc}_q(S)
\quad\text{for every }i,S.
\tag{5}
\]

Necessity follows because an edge can carry any given color at most once. For sufficiency, real separation gives the exact dual condition

\[
\sum_{ij}c_{ij}F_{ij}\ge
\sum_{j<\ell}q_{j\ell}\min_{i\ne k}(c_{ij}+c_{k\ell})
\quad\text{for every real cost matrix }c.
\tag{6}
\]

Subtract the minimum from each column; the shift cancels using the degree equations. Call the new costs \(c'_{ij}\ge0\). Choose a minimizing color \(g_j\) in column \(j\), and let \(\delta_j\) be the second smallest entry in that column, counting ties. For an edge with \(g_j\ne g_\ell\), the minimum edge cost is zero. If the chosen minimizing colors agree, it is \(\min(\delta_j,\delta_\ell)\). Define the nested threshold sets

\[
S_i(t)=\{j:g_j=i,\ \delta_j>t\}.
\]

The full dual deficit in (6) is exactly

\[
\begin{aligned}
&\sum_j\sum_{i\ne g_j}(c'_{ij}-\delta_j)F_{ij}\\
&\qquad+\int_0^\infty\sum_i
\bigl[\operatorname{inc}_q(S_i(t))-F_i(S_i(t))\bigr]dt.
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
\]

To check the identity, use \(\min(u,v)=\int_0^\infty1_{t<u}1_{t<v}\,dt\), and \(\operatorname{inc}_q(S)=\sum_{j\in S}d_j-q(S)\), where (q(S)) is the mass of edges contained in \(S\). Every term in (7) is nonnegative under (5). The integral is a finite sum, since the sets change only at the finitely many \(\delta_j\). This proves the allocation theorem for any number of colors.

### Why actual coisometries satisfy every cut

For a subset \(S\) of right labels, put \(K=\sum_{j\in S}y_jy_j^*\). Then \(0\preceq K\preceq I\), and Cauchy–Binet gives

\[
q^Y(S)=\det K,\qquad
\operatorname{inc}_{q^Y}(S)=\operatorname{tr}K-\det K.
\]

Choose a basis with \(x_i=\sqrt\lambda e_1\), where \(0\le\lambda\le1\), and write

\[
K=\begin{pmatrix}a&z\\\overline z&b\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Then \(F_i(S)=\lambda b\), and the cut deficit has the explicit positive expression

\[
\operatorname{inc}_{q^Y}(S)-F_i(S)
=a(1-b)+(1-\lambda)b+|z|^2\ge0.
\tag{8}
\]

Thus, for arbitrary numbers of parents on both sides, a law reproducing \(F\) and \(q^Y\) always exists. The three-parent assumption is used only in the final step: selecting two of three left labels means

\[
\Pr(\{i,k\}\text{ selected})
=1-\sum_jF_{rj}
=1-\|x_r\|^2=q^X_{ik},
\]

where \(r\) is the omitted label. The last equality is the complementary-minor identity for a unitary completion of \(X\).

Expansion gives

\[
\det(XD_aX^*+YD_bY^*)
=\sum_{i<k}q^X_{ik}a_ia_k
+\sum_{j<\ell}q^Y_{j\ell}b_jb_\ell
+\sum_{ij}F_{ij}a_ib_j.
\]

For an allocated matching with incidences \((i,j),(k,\ell)\), use the product \((a_i+b_\ell)(a_k+b_j)\). Its mixed terms are exactly \(a_ib_j+a_kb_\ell\). Averaging proves (4). Both orientations of every matching are allowed, so this crossing convention does not change the target hull.

### The next compatibility problem is two-dimensional

With four left labels, the pair law is not forced by its degrees. Differences between any two pair laws with equal degrees have the form

\[
\delta q_{12}=\delta q_{34}=s,\quad
\delta q_{13}=\delta q_{24}=t,\quad
\delta q_{14}=\delta q_{23}=-s-t.
\]

The resulting determinant error is

\[
s(a_1-a_3)(a_2-a_4)
+t(a_1-a_2)(a_3-a_4).
\tag{9}
\]

The all-color allocation theorem does not force this error to vanish. The geometric track proves a coarse four-parent merger with the correct double-selection mass and all coarse marginals. A compatible split of that merged parent remains necessary. Independent rounding fails even for some three-parent examples that the correlated theorem already solves.

The complete standalone analytic proof is `geometry_three_color_theorem.md`. Exact earlier enumerations for \(n=4,5,6\) are retained as independent certificates; a planned \(n=8\) enumeration was stopped when the uniform proof superseded it and is not counted as completed.

### Extension to three distinct spectral values with arbitrary multiplicities

The scalar conclusion extends to arbitrary parent counts whenever one spectrum has at most three distinct complex values. Group equal left values as \(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3\), with positive effects

\[
E_i=\sum_{r:a_r=\alpha_i}x_rx_r^*,\qquad \sum_iE_i=I_2.
\]

Put \(d_i=\det E_i\), \(D=\sum_i d_i\), and \(c=1-D\). Reserve a double-selection event in group \(i\) with mass \(d_i\) and right-edge law \(d_iq^Y\). It contributes \(d_i\|y_j\|^2\) to that group's mixed incidence at right vertex \(j\). The residual incidences are represented by

\[
H_i=\operatorname{adj}E_i-d_iI\succeq0,\qquad
\sum_iH_i=cI,\qquad \operatorname{tr}H_i\le c.
\]

For positivity, if the eigenvalues of \(E_i\) are \(e_1,e_2\in[0,1]\), those of \(H_i\) are \(e_2(1-e_1),e_1(1-e_2)\). The trace bound follows from

\[
\det(I-E_i)\ge\sum_{k\ne i}\det E_k.
\]

For every positive contraction \(K\),

\[
\operatorname{tr}(H_iK)
\le c\lambda_{\max}(K)
\le c(\operatorname{tr}K-\det K).
\]

These are exactly the residual colored-edge cuts. Three colors again force the cross-group pair probabilities from their degrees. Together with the reserved double events, the law reproduces the determinant of \(\sum_i\alpha_iE_i+YD_bY^*\).

Every double event of positive mass has \(\det E_i>0\), so its group contains at least two original rank-one columns. Choose distinct labels within it; equal spectral values make the choice immaterial to the scalar product. Thus the law lifts to a valid scalar matching law on the original distinct labels. It need not preserve all fine-label incidences. The full proof is `geometry_three_value_scalar_theorem.md`.


## Exact Clifford coordinates for the unrestricted complex conjecture

### Normalize every nontrivial instance

If some permutation product is nonzero, relabel \(b\) so that \(z_{\mathrm{id}}\ne0\), and set \(m_i=a_i+b_i\). Then every \(m_i\ne0\). If every permutation product vanishes, the Leibniz formula gives

\[
\det((a_i+b_j)U_{ij})=0.
\]

Since \((D_a+UD_bU^*)U=((a_i+b_j)U_{ij})\), the target determinant also vanishes. Thus the normalization covers every nontrivial case exactly.

Define

\[
C_{ij}=\frac{a_i+b_j}{m_i},\qquad
P_i=\frac1{m_i}\begin{pmatrix}b_i&a_ib_i\\1&a_i\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Each \(P_i\) is a complex rank-one idempotent with trace one. Write \(P_i=u_iw_i^T\), where \(u_i=(b_i,1)^T\), \(w_i^T=(1,a_i)/m_i\). Then \(w_i^Tu_i=1\), and \(C_{ij}=w_i^Tu_j\). Multiplication of rank-one matrices gives, for any cycle \(c\),

\[
\operatorname{tr}\prod_{i\in c}P_i
=\prod_{i\in c}C_{i,c(i)}.
\]

Consequently,

\[
\frac{z_\pi}{z_{\mathrm{id}}}
=Z_\pi(P):=\prod_{c\text{ cycle of }\pi}
\operatorname{tr}\prod_{i\in c}P_i,
\qquad
\frac{d(U)}{z_{\mathrm{id}}}
=\frac{\det(C\circ U)}{\det U}.
\tag{10}
\]

### Pauli vectors and the exact converse

Write \(2P_i-I=\nu_i\cdot\sigma\), where \(\sigma\) denotes the three Pauli matrices. Explicitly,

\[
\nu_i=\left(
\frac{1+a_ib_i}{m_i},
\frac{i(a_ib_i-1)}{m_i},
\frac{b_i-a_i}{m_i}
\right),\qquad \nu_i\cdot\nu_i=1.
\]

The dot product here is **complex bilinear**, with no conjugation. Therefore

\[
G_{ij}=\nu_i\cdot\nu_j,\qquad
G^T=G,\quad G_{ii}=1,\quad\operatorname{rank}G\le3.
\tag{11}
\]

For four labels, the rank condition is \(\det G=0\). The oriented volumes must be coherent: if \(V_{ijk}=\nu_i\cdot(\nu_j\times\nu_k)\), then \(V_{ijk}^2=\det G[ijk]\), with all signs coming from the same vector configuration. Pair and triple cycle traces are

\[
s_{ij}:=\operatorname{tr}(P_iP_j)=\frac{1+G_{ij}}2,
\]

\[
\operatorname{tr}(P_iP_jP_k)
=\frac{s_{ij}+s_{jk}+s_{ki}-1}{2}+\frac{i}{4}V_{ijk}.
\]

Every finite collection of rank-one trace-one complex idempotents arises from actual spectral data in this construction. Indeed, one common generic conjugation in \(GL_2(\mathbb C)\) makes all lower-left entries nonzero. Write a transformed idempotent as

\[
P_i=\begin{pmatrix}p_i&q_i\\r_i&1-p_i\end{pmatrix},\qquad r_i\ne0.
\]

Rank one gives \(q_ir_i=p_i(1-p_i)\). Set

\[
a_i=(1-p_i)/r_i,\qquad b_i=p_i/r_i.
\]

These reconstruct \(P_i\) exactly. Simultaneous conjugation preserves every cycle trace. A complex symmetric \(G\) satisfying (11) admits a complex bilinear Gram factorization, which supplies the corresponding idempotents and coherent orientations.

Thus the following abstract assertion is **equivalent to complex MOC in every dimension**:

\[
\frac{\det(C\circ U)}{\det U}
\in\operatorname{conv}_{\mathbb R}\{Z_\pi(P):\pi\in S_n\}
\tag{12}
\]

for every such idempotent family and every \(U\in U(n)\). This is an exact change of mathematical language with a converse; positivity in (12) remains to be proved.

### A real Gram support theorem and an exact limitation

When \(G\) is real symmetric, \(\zeta=\eta z_{\mathrm{id}}>0\) is real, and the identity maximizes the permutation support, the required support inequality is proved in every dimension.

If \(G\succeq0\), choose real Pauli vectors and Hermitian rank-one projectors \(P_i=x_ix_i^*\). The multiplier \(C\) is a correlation matrix, and the isometry \(Ve_i=e_i\otimes x_i\) gives

\[
C\circ U=V^*(U\otimes I_2)V.
\]

Its singular values are at most one, proving \(|d(U)/z_{\mathrm{id}}|\le1\), hence the radial support inequality.

If \(G\) has at most two positive and at most one negative direction, it has a realization \(\nu_i=(x_i,iy_i,z_i)\) with real coordinates. The resulting \(P_i\) are real. A real common conjugation reconstructs real spectra, and Fiedler's Hermitian determinant theorem proves full convex-hull membership for this normalized branch. Rank-deficient cases are handled by matching cycle traces, not by assuming an isometry of possibly degenerate spans.

The only remaining real rank-three inertia, \((1,2)\), cannot occur under the stated support assumptions. Transposition gaps require \(G_{ij}\le1\). On either sheet of the unit timelike hyperboloid, the Lorentz inner product is at least one, with equality only for identical vectors. Thus at most two distinct vectors could occur, contradicting rank three.


## Abstract complex MOC in Jordan rank at most three

Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional Euclidean Jordan algebra of rank \(r\), and let \((e_i)\), \((f_j)\) be real Jordan frames. Extend the product, trace, and determinant to the complexification. For arbitrary complex coefficients, set

\[
x=\sum_i a_i e_i,\qquad y=\sum_j b_j f_j,
\qquad P_{ij}=\operatorname{tr}(e_i\circ f_j).
\]

The overlap matrix \(P\) is doubly stochastic. Positivity follows from the Peirce operator \(L_{f_j}\), whose eigenvalues belong to \(\{0,1/2,1\}\):

\[
P_{ij}=\langle e_i,L_{f_j}e_i\rangle\ge0.
\]

Summing either index gives one. Choose a Birkhoff decomposition \(P=\sum_\pi w_\pi P_\pi\). In rank three the cubic polarization identity is

\[
\det(x+y)=\det x+\det y+\langle x^\#,y\rangle+\langle x,y^\#\rangle.
\]

On a frame, \(x^\#=\sum_i(\prod_{k\ne i}a_k)e_i\). Substitution gives

\[
\det(x+y)=\prod_i a_i+\prod_jb_j
+\sum_{ij}P_{ij}\left[
\Bigl(\prod_{k\ne i}a_k\Bigr)b_j
+a_i\Bigl(\prod_{\ell\ne j}b_\ell\Bigr)\right].
\]

Inserting the Birkhoff decomposition proves

\[
\boxed{\det(x+y)=\sum_\pi w_\pi\prod_i(a_i+b_{\pi(i)})}.
\tag{13}
\]

The same nonnegative weights work for all complex spectra. The proof uses trace associativity and Jordan polarization, so it includes the complexification of the exceptional Albert algebra of three-by-three Hermitian octonionic matrices.

For a rank-two spin factor, write \(x=(s,tn)\), \(y=(u,vm)\), with real unit vectors \(n,m\) and complex scalars \(s,t,u,v\). Its determinant gives

\[
\det(x+y)
=\frac{1+n\cdot m}{2}\bigl[(s+u)^2-(t+v)^2\bigr]
+\frac{1-n\cdot m}{2}\bigl[(s+u)^2-(t-v)^2\bigr].
\tag{14}
\]

Both weights are nonnegative. This proves the appropriate complex MOC in every Clifford spin factor. The determinant rank remains two; (14) is not an assertion about arbitrary full matrix determinants of Clifford representations. Products of Jordan factors of rank at most three are covered by multiplying their positive laws.

An irreducible rank-four algebra introduces a mixed determinant term of degree \((2,2)\). Frame overlaps control the \((3,1)\) and \((1,3)\) terms, but not that middle term. In the matrix model the missing data are the squared two-minors. This is the exact failure of a direct low-rank induction. The complete proof is in `root_jordan_rank_three.md`. The known real-spectrum Jordan Fiedler theorem is due to Sossa; see [the 2023 primary paper](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0024379523000952).

## The exact coupled quartic still required for MOC in dimension four

Real skew-symmetric Cayley generators now suffice by realification. Any real orthogonal matrix can enter a Cayley chart after row sign changes, which preserve squared minors: averaging \(\det(I+DO)\) over diagonal sign matrices \(D\) gives one, so at least one is nonzero.

Fix actual complex \(a,b,\eta\), relabel \(b\) so the identity achieves \(h=\operatorname{Re}(\eta z_{\mathrm{id}})\), and put \(\delta_\pi=h-\operatorname{Re}(\eta z_\pi)\ge0\). For real skew \(K\), set

\[
U_t=(I+tK)(I-tK)^{-1},\qquad S=D_a+D_b.
\]

Clearing the denominator gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
P_K(t)&=h\det(I-tK)^2\\
&\quad-\operatorname{Re}\left\{\eta\det\bigl(S+t[D_a-D_b,K]-t^2KSK\bigr)\right\}\\
&=t^2\bigl(q_0+q_1t+q_2t^2+q_3t^3+q_4t^4\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\tag{21}
\]

The first two coefficients are

\[
q_0=4\sum_{i<j}\delta_{ij}K_{ij}^2,
\qquad
q_1=8\sum_{i<j<k}K_{ij}K_{jk}K_{ki}
\bigl(\delta_{(ijk)}-\delta_{(ikj)}\bigr).
\tag{22}
\]

With \(\rho=\operatorname{Pf}K\) and \(*K\) the Hodge dual,

\[
q_3=\rho q_1(*K),\qquad
q_4=\rho^2q_0(*K),\qquad q_2(*K)=q_2(K).
\tag{23}
\]

For a pairing \(ij\mid k\ell\), define \(B_{ij\mid k\ell}=8(2\delta_{(ij)(k\ell)}-\delta_{ij}-\delta_{k\ell})\). These three \(B\)'s are residue coefficients, distinct from the simpler capacity bound above. Order them by \(12\mid34,13\mid24,14\mid23\), put

\[
r=(K_{12}K_{34},-K_{13}K_{24},K_{14}K_{23}),
\]

and let \(H_{ii}=B_i\), \(H_{ij}=(B_i+B_j-B_k)/2\) for distinct \(i,j,k\). Then

\[
q_2=4\sum_{i<j}\delta_{ij}\sum_{k\ne i,j}K_{ik}^2K_{kj}^2+r^THr.
\tag{24}
\]

The missing universal implication is that the quartic in (21) is nonnegative on the real line for every **actual spectral** coefficient system (22)–(24). Along with realification, this would prove MOC in dimension four. It would not yet prove every dimension.

Every real nonnegative univariate quartic is a sum of squares of real quadratics. Here that is equivalent to the existence of a real \(z\) with

\[
\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}
q_0&q_1/2&z\\
q_1/2&q_2-2z&q_3/2\\
z&q_3/2&q_4
\end{pmatrix}\succeq0.}
\tag{25}
\]

For \(q_0q_4>0\), whenever a feasible Gram parameter exists, one can choose

\[
z_*=(q_2-\sqrt{q_2^2-3q_1q_3+12q_0q_4})/6.
\]

This canonical branch follows by maximizing the cubic Gram determinant and treating boundaries by regularization. The unproved step is that actual spectral constraints always make (25) feasible.

Principal three-index restrictions have exact SOS certificates, using

\[
\delta_{(ijk)}+\delta_{(ikj)}
=\delta_{ij}+\delta_{ik}+\delta_{jk}.
\]


If \(\operatorname{Pf}K=0\), the remaining target is the quadratic inequality

\[
q_0+q_1t+q_2t^2\ge0,
\quad\text{equivalently }q_0,q_2\ge0,\quad q_1^2\le4q_0q_2.
\tag{26}
\]

A qualification about the supporting permutation matters here. The
rank-two statement is made after the identity has been chosen as a
maximizing permutation. Relabeling columns to normalize an arbitrary support
direction need not preserve the rank-two Cayley family. A proof of (26)
for all such normalized rays would therefore solve that precise support
obligation; it should not automatically be described as MOC for every
fixed matrix in a globally permutation-invariant rank-two class.


A geometric identity retains that information. Write \(K=XJX^T\), with rows \(X_i=(x_i,y_i)\), and set

\[
V_i=(x_i^2,x_iy_i,y_i^2),\quad
S_i=(1,b_i-a_i,a_ib_i),\quad m_i=a_i+b_i.
\]

Then, with \(\zeta=\eta\prod_i m_i\),

\[
q_1=8\operatorname{Re}\left\{
\zeta\det\bigl(S^T\operatorname{diag}(1/m_i)V\bigr)\right\}.
\tag{27}
\]

This follows from \(K_{ij}K_{jk}K_{ki}=-\det V[ijk]\), the spectral cycle identity, and Cauchy–Binet. It pairs a real Veronese conic with the complex spectral conic in a three-by-three determinant. An estimate controlling it by \(q_0q_2\) would close (26); the identity alone does not supply that estimate.

### The rank-two middle coefficient has an exact spectral determinant formula

For \(K=XJX^T\), define the two plane determinants

\[
F_a=\det[X,D_aX],\qquad F_b=\det[X,D_bX].
\]

Here each concatenated matrix is four by four. The exact identity is

\[
\boxed{q_2=
4\sum_{i<j}\delta_{ij}\bigl((K^2)_{ij}\bigr)^2
-16\operatorname{Re}(\eta F_aF_b).}
\tag{27a}
\]

The square in the first term is an entry of \(K^2\) squared, not an entry
of \(K\) raised to the fourth power.
To derive the identity, orthonormalize the two columns of \(X\) and use
the determinant lemma on the endpoint reflection. The normalized
determinant becomes \(\det(I_4-2N)\), where \(N\) is a four by four
matrix with \(\operatorname{tr}N=2\) and \(N-I_4/2\) Hamiltonian.
Consequently its elementary symmetric functions satisfy
\(e_3=e_2-1\), and

\[
\det(I_4-2N)=-3+2\operatorname{tr}(N^2)+16\det N.
\]

The determinant of \(N\) is
\(F_aF_b/(\sigma^2 z_{\rm id})\), where
\(\sigma=\det(X^TX)\). Substitution and homogeneity give (27a);
degenerate cases follow polynomially. Independent review derived the
same identity from the original determinant.

In particular, put

\[
\mathcal A=\sum_{i<j}\delta_{ij}K_{ij}^2,\qquad
\mathcal B=\operatorname{Re}
\{\zeta\det(S^T\operatorname{diag}(1/m_i)V)\},
\]
\[
\mathcal C=
\sum_{i<j}\delta_{ij}\bigl((K^2)_{ij}\bigr)^2
-4\operatorname{Re}(\eta F_aF_b).
\]

Then the rank-two obligation is exactly

\[
\boxed{\mathcal C\ge0,\qquad \mathcal B^2\le\mathcal A\mathcal C.}
\tag{27b}
\]

One proved slice is
\(\mathcal B=0\) and \(\operatorname{Re}(\eta F_aF_b)\le0\).
The general inequalities in (27b) remain unproved.
The analysis track also extends the plane determinant-lemma reduction
to arbitrary matrix dimension: the auxiliary Hamiltonian matrix still
has size four, and the \(F_aF_b\) term becomes a sum over four-label
subsets with the complementary factors \(\prod_{j\notin I}m_j\).
This exact dimensional compression of a rotation trajectory supplies
no universal positivity conclusion by itself.

## A second exact proof template with a rank-one dual certificate

### The coefficient hull equivalent to MOC in dimension four

For \(C=U\operatorname{diag}(\beta)U^*\), define

\[
D_0=\det C[124]+\det C[14],\qquad
D_1=C_{11}+\det C[12].
\]

Let

\[
v_\pi=\bigl(\beta_{\pi(1)}(1+\beta_{\pi(2)})\beta_{\pi(4)},
\ \beta_{\pi(1)}(1+\beta_{\pi(2)})\bigr).
\]

The statement \((D_0,D_1)\in\operatorname{conv}_{\mathbb R}\{v_\pi\}\subset\mathbb C^2\) for every \(\beta,U\) is equivalent to MOC in dimension four. One direction follows by applying MOC to \(\operatorname{diag}(0,1,M,t)+C\), dividing by \(M\), and letting real \(M\to\infty\): the limit is \(D_0+tD_1\). Varying complex \(t\) and the support phase supplies every real functional on \(\mathbb C^2\).

For the reverse direction, a simultaneous fractional-linear spectral normalization preserves \(U\) and multiplies the determinant and every matching product by one common nonzero scalar. Explicitly, for generic spectra,

\[
\kappa=\frac{a_2-a_3}{a_2-a_1},\qquad
\beta_j=-\kappa\frac{b_j+a_1}{b_j+a_3},\qquad
t=\kappa\frac{a_4-a_1}{a_4-a_3},
\]

\[
\Gamma=-\frac{(a_2-a_1)(a_4-a_3)}{\kappa^2(a_3-a_1)^2}
\prod_j(a_3+b_j),
\qquad d(U)=\Gamma(D_0+tD_1).
\]

Every permutation product has the same factor \(\Gamma\). Degenerate spectra follow by continuity. The earlier report also gives an explicit finite spectral recovery of any strict coefficient-hull violation, so this equivalence is not based only on a formal limiting analogy.

### The exact fixed-row SDP

Fix a real unit row \(v\in\mathbb R^4\), and let \(H=v^\perp\). Write

\[
m=\sum_j\beta_jv_j^2,\quad w=D_\beta v,\quad p=\prod_j\beta_j,
\qquad T_\beta=(1+m)D_\beta-ww^T.
\]

With the row effects from the realification theorem,

\[
D_1=\operatorname{tr}(T_\beta E_1),\qquad
D_0=p\operatorname{tr}(T_{1/\beta}E_3).
\]

The reciprocal expression is polynomial after cancellation, so zero \(\beta_j\) present no gap. For the support direction \((\lambda_0,\lambda_1)\), restrict the real symmetric matrices

\[
M=\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_1T_\beta),\qquad
N=\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_0pT_{1/\beta})
\]

to \(H\). The maximum over all orthogonal completions of \(v\) is exactly

\[
f(M,N)=\max\{\operatorname{tr}(ME)+\operatorname{tr}(NF):
E,F,G\succeq0,\ E+F+G=I_3, 
\operatorname{tr}E=\operatorname{tr}F=\operatorname{tr}G=1\}.
\tag{28}
\]

Exactness follows from the real three-effect lemma: this feasible set is precisely the convex hull of orthonormal frames. The dual is

\[
\min\{\operatorname{tr}Y+\alpha+\gamma:
Y\succeq0,\ Y+\alpha I\succeq M,\ Y+\gamma I\succeq N\}.
\tag{29}
\]

There is no duality gap; the primal point \(E=F=G=I_3/3\) is strictly feasible.

### An optimal shared dual of rank at most one

Choose an optimal primal orthonormal frame \(r,s,t\) and an optimal dual \(Y,\alpha,\gamma\). Complementarity gives \(Yt=0\). Thus \(Y\) has rank at most two. If its rank is two, let \(\tau>0\) be the smaller positive eigenvalue on \(t^\perp\), and replace

\[
Y\longmapsto Y-\tau(I-tt^T),\qquad
\alpha\longmapsto\alpha+\tau,\qquad
\gamma\longmapsto\gamma+\tau.
\]

The new \(Y\) is positive semidefinite with rank at most one. Each of the other two slacks gains \(\tau tt^T\), so remains positive semidefinite. The objective is unchanged: \(-2\tau+\tau+\tau=0\). Therefore the exact attained formula is

\[
\boxed{f(M,N)=\min_{y\in\mathbb R^3}
\left[\|y\|^2+\lambda_{\max}(M-yy^T)+\lambda_{\max}(N-yy^T)\right].}
\tag{30}
\]

This is valid for every pair of real symmetric three-by-three matrices. For MOC, the required bound is

\[
\boxed{\exists y:\quad
\|y\|^2+\lambda_{\max}(M(v)-yy^T)+\lambda_{\max}(N(v)-yy^T)
\le\max_\pi\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_0v_{\pi,0}+\lambda_1v_{\pi,1}).}
\tag{31}
\]

It must hold for every actual \(\beta,\lambda_0,\lambda_1,v\). This is another exact remaining proof obligation for dimension four. Formula (30) proves that searching for a common rank-one correction loses nothing. It does not construct a correction meeting the spectral bound (31).

### What the coupled spectra retain

The two matrices in (31) are related through reciprocal compressions. If \(P_H\) projects onto \(H\), let

\[
C_\beta=P_HD_\beta P_H|_H,\qquad
C_\gamma=P_HD_{1/\beta}P_H|_H.
\]

Schur-block identities give

\[
T_\beta|_H=C_\beta+p\operatorname{adj}C_\gamma,\qquad
pT_{1/\beta}|_H=pC_\gamma+\operatorname{adj}C_\beta,
\]

and \(\operatorname{rank}(C_\beta C_\gamma-I)\le1\). These are exact algebraic couplings that independent eigenvalue estimates discard.

There is an additional exact polynomial coupling. Put
\[
B=T_\beta|_H,\qquad \mathcal N=pT_{1/\beta}|_H,\qquad
s=1+m,\qquad
t=p+\sum_jv_j^2\prod_{k\ne j}\beta_k.
\]
The canceled expression for \(\mathcal N\) is polynomial, including
zero spectral values. Schur complements and adjugates give
\[
\boxed{
B^\#=s\mathcal N,\quad
\mathcal N^\#=tB,\quad
B\mathcal N=\mathcal NB=stI_H,\quad
\det B=s^2t,\quad \det\mathcal N=st^2.
}
\]
Thus the two complex pencils are scalar inverses wherever invertible.
The identities hold on singular strata by polynomial continuation.
Their real support parts need not commute in general.

When \(|\beta_j|=1\), however,
\(\mathcal N=p\overline B\), and the reciprocal identity gives
\[
BB^*=|1+m|^2I_H.
\]
Since \(B\) is complex symmetric, its real and imaginary parts then
commute. Both real support matrices commute for every support direction
on this branch.

The ordinary coefficient-hull assertion for this entire unit-circle
branch is already a consequence of Shitov's 2025 theorem. Choose a
circle-to-line Möbius map \(T\) with an avoided pole. For the finite
spectra \(a=(0,1,M,u)\), the transformed spectrum
\(-T(-\beta_j)\) is real and \(T(a_2)=T(1)\) is real, leaving at most
three nonreal entries on the other side. Shitov applies. Undo the
transformation and take the coefficient normalization limit
\(M\to\infty\). This proves the ordinary hull assertion, not the
stronger assertion with prescribed row marginals.

The transformation used here can be verified directly. If
\[
T(z)=\frac{Az+B_0}{Cz+D},\qquad AD-B_0C\ne0,
\]
then the transformed sum determinant and every permutation product are
multiplied by the same nonzero factor
\[
\frac{(AD-B_0C)^4}
{\prod_i(Ca_i+D)\prod_j(D-C\beta_j)}.
\]
Thus real convex-hull membership is unchanged. All required poles can
be avoided before taking limits.


Each single pencil has a proved conditional vertex bound using an oriented two-row matching law. Coupling the two laws is the difficult step. Several stronger shortcuts are false: the exact fixed-row optimum need not be convex in \((v_j^2)\), and one common rank-one correction need not cancel both indefinite rank-two terms by itself. The valid dual allows spectral diagonal slack, which those simpler ansatzes remove.

On every outer-simplex edge, where \(v\) has at most two nonzero coordinates, both pencils are diagonal in a common natural basis and the joint assignment certificate is complete. The corresponding unitary class has a full common minor law in dimension four. Successful boundary cases do not establish (31) for interior \(v\).

## How these reductions connect to unrestricted dimension

Define \(\mathrm{CMC}(r;m,n)\) as the assertion that

\[
\det(XD_aX^*+YD_bY^*)
\]

belongs to the hull of products \(\prod_{k=1}^r(a_{i_k}+b_{j_k})\), with distinct selected indices on either side, for coisometries of sizes \(r\times m\) and \(r\times n\). A scalar law may depend on the spectra; a common law must work unchanged for all spectra.

There is an exact dimension reduction:

\[
\boxed{\mathrm{MOC}(n)\ \Longleftrightarrow 
\mathrm{CMC}(n-1;n-1,n).}
\tag{32}
\]

To prove it, first take

\[
A=\operatorname{diag}(a_1,\ldots,a_r,c,\ldots,c),\quad
\alpha_i=1/(a_i-c),\quad \beta_j=1/(c+b_j),
\]

and let \(X\) be the first \(r\) rows of \(U\). The determinant lemma gives

\[
\det(A+UD_bU^*)=
\Gamma\det(D_\alpha+XD_\beta X^*),\qquad
\Gamma=\prod_{i=1}^r(a_i-c)\prod_j(c+b_j).
\tag{33}
\]

For every injection \(j:[r]\to[n]\), the same scalar transforms vertices:

\[
\Gamma\prod_{i=1}^r(\alpha_i+\beta_{j(i)})
=\prod_{i=1}^r(a_i+b_{j(i)})\prod_{k\notin j([r])}(c+b_k).
\]

These are full permutation products. Set \(r=n-1,c=a_n\) to obtain one direction of (32). Conversely, complete any \((n-1)\times n\) coisometry to a unitary, apply MOC to a diagonal matrix with one entry \(M\), divide by positive \(M\), and let \(M\to\infty\). The limit gives the compressed determinant and its vertex hull. All excluded poles in (33) follow by continuity.

For dimension four, the required scalar compression is therefore \(\mathrm{CMC}(3;3,4)\). The proved rank-two theorem \(\mathrm{CMC}(2;3,n)\) is substantial, but is not that rank-three assertion. Moreover a spectrum-independent common law for \(\mathrm{CMC}(3;3,4)\) would recover the full squared-minor law of a unitary completion, through complementary minors. That stronger law has exact counterexamples. Any successful lifting must preserve only the required scalar, or use spectrum-dependent weights.

For scalar rank-two compression, an additional theorem reduces all possible finite counterexamples to at most eight nonzero columns on each side. Fix a strict separating support direction, vary nonnegative weights on the original rank-one rays subject to their sum being \(I_2\), and choose a maximizing weight vector of minimal support \(s\). Its tangent dimension is at least \(s-4\). The determinant objective is a quadratic whose real part has negative index at most four, because \(\operatorname{Re}(\eta\det Z)\) on \(M_2(\mathbb C)\) has signature \((4,4)\). If \(s>8\), a nonzero null tangent at the maximum makes the exact quadratic constant along a feasible line. Moving to an endpoint deletes a ray, a contradiction. Repeating on the other frame proves the \((8,8)\) reduction. For real rays the dimensions and indices are both three, giving \((6,6)\). This theorem is proved in `root_rank_two_finite_reduction.md`; it is a scalar reduction, not a reduction of common laws.

Thus a complete all-dimensional proof still requires a universal step for the sequence of scalar compression problems in (32), or a direct proof of the Clifford assertion (12). Neither the Jordan low-rank identities nor the many solved spectral boundary classes supplies that missing implication automatically.
