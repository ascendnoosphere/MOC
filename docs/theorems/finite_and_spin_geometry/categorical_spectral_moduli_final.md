# The actual spectral-cost variety for complex MOC

## Status and main results

This note concerns the **costs in the scalar supporting-hyperplane formulation** of the Marcus–de Oliveira conjecture. It does not prove unrestricted complex MOC and does not supply a counterexample to it. It identifies the exact algebraic restriction that is lost when arbitrary linear costs are substituted for determinant spectral costs.

The principal results established here are:

1. Four-by-four rank-two permutation products are explicitly the fourteen invariant coordinates of eight points on a projective line. Every point of the complex invariant cone has a realization by finite complex spectra, with an allowed common scalar.
2. In explicit MOC coordinates, the eight-point skew cubic has 49 terms. Its derivative in the constant-cost direction is exactly the determinant of a natural two-by-two singlet block.
3. After removing constant costs, the Zariski closure of the **actual complex spectral support costs** is an irreducible hypersurface of **degree nine**. Its entire polynomial ideal is generated by that nonic. In particular there is no nonzero polynomial relation of degree less than nine.
4. Membership in the actual real cost image has an exact real Hessian-kernel/quadratic test. The real secant equation alone is insufficient. Explicit finite spectral examples distinguish conjugate-only, split-only, and overlapping real representations.
5. Every symmetry copy of the nontrivial common-minor facet normal is excluded from the actual spectral cost image, even after arbitrary constant shifts and nonzero scalings. A certified open neighborhood is excluded as well.
6. There are concrete Lorentz Gram and higher-dimensional Pfaffian constructions. They give positive identities and higher-order spectral relations, but a positivity argument strong enough for the MOC support inequality is still missing.

All computations use integer or rational arithmetic. No floating-point numerical evidence is used as a proof in this note. The exact programs and certificate files are listed in Section 12.

## 1. The finite spectral realization and the invariant quotient

Write

\[
z_\pi=\prod_{i=1}^4(a_i+b_{\pi(i)}),\qquad
c_\pi=\Re(\eta z_\pi),\qquad \pi\in S_4.
\tag{1}
\]

The scalar MOC question is whether, for every unitary \(U\),

\[
\Re\{\eta\det(A+UBU^*)\}\le \max_\pi c_\pi.
\tag{2}
\]

For spinors \(p_i\in\mathbb C^2\), put \([ij]=\det(p_i,p_j)\). Let the first four labels correspond to rows and the last four to columns. The rank-two matrix

\[
C_{ij}=[i,4+j]
\]

has permutation products \(m_\pi=\prod_i[i,4+\pi(i)]\). Conversely, every rank-at-most-two matrix factors in this bracket form: write \(C=XY\), identify each row covector of \(X\) with a spinor by the nondegenerate alternating form, and use the columns of \(Y\).

For a perfect matching \(M\) of eight labels, define \(m_M=\prod_{\{i,j\}\in M}[ij]\), orienting each edge increasingly. The Plücker relation

\[
[ij][kl]-[ik][jl]+[il][jk]=0
\tag{3}
\]

replaces a pair of within-family edges by cross-family edges. Every matching has the same number of edges entirely in each family; induction therefore expresses every matching as a linear combination of bipartite permutation matchings. These coordinates give the standard invariant quotient

\[
M_8=(\mathbb P^1)^8//SL_2\subset\mathbb P^{13}.
\]

**Finite-chart lemma.** Every nonzero point of the affine invariant cone is an actual finite complex spectral product vector, up to a common complex scalar. The projective quotient is surjective on semistable configurations. Take eight nonzero representing spinors. Choose one common \(g\in SL_2(\mathbb C)\) whose second-coordinate linear form vanishes at none of these finitely many points. Then

\[
gp_i=\lambda_i(a_i,1),\qquad
gp_{4+j}=\mu_j(-b_j,1),
\]

with finite \(a_i,b_j\) and nonzero \(\lambda_i,\mu_j\). Thus

\[
m_\pi=\kappa\prod_i(a_i+b_{\pi(i)}),\qquad
\kappa=\prod_i\lambda_i\prod_j\mu_j\ne0.
\tag{4}
\]

The scalar \(\kappa\) is absorbed in \(\eta\). Alternatively, over \(\mathbb C\), multiplying all spectra by a fourth root of \(\kappa\) absorbs it into the products themselves. The zero cone point is trivial. The use of degree-one invariant generators identifies the affine cone with this quotient cone; no additional limit of infinite spectral parameters is required.

The same argument works for \(2n\) points, with the two families of size \(n\). The coordinate dimension is the Catalan number \(C_n\), the projective quotient dimension is \(2n-3\), and the affine complex dimension is \(2n-2\) for \(n\ge2\). This is an exact invariant description of the scalar spectral tests; it supplies no automatic positivity under insertion of further labels.

## 2. An explicit fourteen-coordinate model

Use the following permutation products as coordinates \(x_0,\ldots,x_{13}\), in this order:

\[
1234,1243,1324,1342,1423,2134,2143,
2314,2341,2413,3124,3142,3412,4123.
\tag{5}
\]

The program expands each matching into monomials
\(\prod_{i\in I}u_i\prod_{i\notin I}v_i\), \(|I|=4\), where \(p_i=(u_i,v_i)\). There are seventy such monomials. Rational elimination proves that the fourteen selected columns are independent and span all twenty-four permutation columns and all 105 matching columns. Thus every matching is a specified linear form \(\ell_M(x)\). The certificate records all 105 coefficient vectors, with no implicit choice of coordinates.

Let \(\epsilon(M)\) be the sign of the permutation obtained by listing the increasingly oriented pairs of \(M\) consecutively. Define

\[
F(x)=\frac16\sum_{M}\epsilon(M)\ell_M(x)^3.
\tag{6}
\]

This is nonzero and has 49 terms in (5). Its value on an eight-spinor configuration is a scalar multiple of

\[
\operatorname{Pf}\bigl([ij]^3\bigr)_{i,j=1}^8=0:
\]

the matrix is the alternating Gram matrix of the vectors \(p_i^{\otimes3}\) in the four-dimensional space \(\operatorname{Sym}^3(\mathbb C^2)\), so its rank is at most four. The exact program additionally composes all fourteen quadratic derivatives of (6) with the seventy-monomial parametrization and verifies that all coefficients vanish.

The known input from Howard–Millson–Snowden–Vakil is that this skew cubic has \(M_8\) as its scheme-theoretic singular locus; its fourteen derivatives generate the ideal of \(M_8\); and its Hessian divisor on the cubic is twice the irreducible secant variety. The latter has projective dimension eleven. These results, and the matching-cube description, are in their primary paper, Sections 1–4, especially Remark 3.2, Corollary 3.12, Proposition 3.19, and Corollary 4.2: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1103.5255 . The calculations below use these results but do not claim priority for the eight-point geometry.

## 3. The singlet determinant and the special projection center

Define the natural singlet block by

\[
B(x)=\begin{pmatrix}
[12][34][56][78]&[12][34][57][68]\\
[13][24][56][78]&[13][24][57][68]
\end{pmatrix},
\tag{7}
\]

where each displayed matching is interpreted as its linear form in \(x\). In the coordinates (5), its four entries are

\[
\begin{aligned}
B_{00}&=x_0-x_1-x_5+x_6,\\
B_{01}&=x_2-x_3-x_{10}+x_{11},\\
B_{10}&=x_2-x_4-x_7+x_9,\\
B_{11}&=-x_0+x_1+2x_2-x_3-x_4+x_5-x_7-x_{10}+x_{12}.
\end{aligned}\tag{8}
\]

For an actual complex point, (7) is an outer product and has rank at most one. It is a coordinate model of the \((2,2)\) Schur–Weyl block; no choice of an orthonormal Specht basis is needed here.

Let \(e=(1,\ldots,1)\). The four-plus-four collision configuration gives this point of \(M_8\). Direct exact differentiation gives the particularly simple identity

\[
\boxed{D_eF(x)=\det B(x)=:Q(x).}\tag{9}
\]

The scalar in (9) is exactly one for the normalization (6). Since \(B(e)=0\),

\[
F(x+te)=F(x)+tQ(x),\qquad H_F(e)\text{ has rank }4.
\tag{10}
\]

Thus a constant cost can be eliminated by a linear equation, despite the cubic ambient geometry.

## 4. The projected secant is a nonic hypersurface

This section proves the degree, rather than inferring it from numerical interpolation.

Choose exact linear coordinates \(x=T(t,a,b)\), where \(a\in\mathbb C^9\), \(b=(b_{00},b_{01},b_{10},b_{11})\), \(b=B(x)\), and changing \(t\) changes \(x\) by \(e\). The explicit rational transformation in the certificate has determinant \(-1\). In these coordinates,

\[
F=tQ(b)+C(a,b),\qquad Q=b_{00}b_{11}-b_{01}b_{10}.
\tag{11}
\]

Write \(J=H_Q\), \(\ell=Jb\), \(K=C_{aa}\), \(L=C_{ab}\), and \(M=C_{bb}\). The Hessian is

\[
\mathcal H(t)=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&0&\ell^T\\
0&K&L\\
\ell&L^T&tJ+M
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{12}
\]

Here \(\det J=1\). On the dense open set where \(K\) is invertible, a Schur complement gives

\[
\det\mathcal H(t)
=-\det K\;\ell^T\operatorname{adj}(tJ+M-L^TK^{-1}L)\ell.
\]

This is a polynomial of degree at most three in \(t\), and its leading coefficient is

\[
h_3=-\det K\det J\;\ell^TJ^{-1}\ell=-2Q\det K.
\tag{13}
\]

The identities hold everywhere by polynomial continuation. Put
\(\det\mathcal H(t)=h_0+h_1t+h_2t^2+h_3t^3\). The following expression is a homogeneous polynomial of degree eighteen in the thirteen quotient coordinates:

\[
\boxed{N=Q^2h_0-CQh_1+C^2h_2+2C^3\det K.}\tag{14}
\]

On \(Q\ne0\), it equals \(Q^2\det\mathcal H(-C/Q)\). Projection from \(e\) identifies this open subset of the cubic with an open subset of the thirteen-dimensional affine quotient: its inverse is \(t=-C/Q\). By the HMSV Hessian-divisor theorem, every zero component of \(N\) on this open set is the irreducible projected secant, with multiplicity two.

The only possible extra irreducible factor outside this open set is \(Q\), which is irreducible. It does not occur. An exact witness is

\[
t=0,\quad a=(1,-2,3,0,1,2,-1,4,2),\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]

At this point,

\[
Q=0,\quad C=8,\quad \det K=-34560,
\]

\[
(h_0,h_1,h_2,h_3)=(-1396736,1231872,-193536,0),
\]

and

\[
N=-47775744=-6912^2\ne0.
\tag{15}
\]

Consequently \(N\) is a nonzero constant times the square of the irreducible projected-secant equation. Degree eighteen gives **degree nine** for that equation. It is unnecessary to expand a large nonic to represent it exactly: (12)–(14) and the explicit coordinate transformation are a determinantal specification.

With the normalization that the nonic \(P_9\) takes value one at the centered facet point of Section 7, the constant is \(-4\), so

\[
N=-4P_9^2.
\tag{16}
\]

### The minimal polynomial relation for actual costs

The actual real-part image is Zariski dense in the real secant variety. Indeed, if a polynomial vanishes on \(\Re m(p)\) for every complex spinor configuration, write \(p=u+iv\). Polynomial identity in the real variables \(u,v\) extends to complex \(u,v\). The invertible change to independent variables \(p=u+iv\), \(q=u-iv\) then shows that it vanishes on \((m(p)+m(q))/2\) for independent configurations. Their closure is the secant cone. Apply the same argument after the linear projection by constants.

It follows that the entire polynomial vanishing ideal of actual centered complex spectral costs is

\[
\boxed{I=\langle P_9\rangle.}\tag{17}
\]

In particular, no degree-one through degree-eight polynomial equality can describe an additional spectral restriction. Inequalities and real component conditions are still essential.

## 5. An exact real membership test

For a real cubic and real vectors \(c,d\), polarization gives

\[
\nabla F(c+id)=\nabla F(c)-\nabla F(d)+iH_F(c)d.
\]

Combining the singular-locus theorem with the finite-chart lemma yields

\[
\boxed{
c=\Re z\text{ for an actual finite complex spectral cost vector}
\iff
\exists d\in\mathbb R^{14}:
H_F(c)d=0,\quad \nabla F(d)=\nabla F(c).
}\tag{18}
\]

This includes zero. It is an explicit real linear-kernel calculation followed by a quadratic feasibility problem, not a replacement of conjugate secants by all real secants.

For a quotient cost \(u\), when \(Q(u)\ne0\), the only possible constant lift is

\[
c=u-\frac{F(u)}{Q(u)}e.\tag{19}
\]

If \(Q=0,F\ne0\), no lift exists. If \(Q=F=0\), the scalar lift parameter must be retained in (18). This exceptional case is not discarded.

There is a corresponding split test. A real vector is the sum of two real points of the invariant cone if and only if there is a real \(d\) with

\[
H_F(c)d=0,\qquad \nabla F(d)=-\nabla F(c).
\tag{20}
\]

The endpoints are \((c+d)/2\) and \((c-d)/2\). Every sum of two ordinary real spectral product vectors is included in (20). The term “split” in this section refers precisely to these two real invariant-cone endpoints; it does not presume that all real projective secants have a prescribed affine sign.

### A two-variable certificate on the regular examples

Suppose the Hessian kernel has dimension two, with basis \(d,k\), and \(g=\nabla F(c)\ne0\). Suppose also that \(\nabla F(d)=\varepsilon g\), \(\varepsilon=\pm1\), and the three vectors \(g,H_F(d)k,\nabla F(k)\) span a two-dimensional space. Choosing two coordinates that distinguish that span, the condition that \(\nabla F(sd+tk)\) be proportional to \(g\) becomes exactly

\[
t(\alpha s+\beta t)=0,\qquad \alpha\ne0.\tag{21}
\]

Thus there are exactly two projective kernel directions to check. The sign of the proportionality coefficient decides whether that direction admits the positive normalization (18), the negative normalization (20), or neither. All rank and proportionality hypotheses are checked exactly in the examples below; they are not assumed for every singular or degenerate cost.

## 6. The two real forms are incomparable and can overlap

The following examples use finite spectra. The fourteen-coordinate vectors use (5). All listed \(Q\)'s are nonzero, so their constant lifts are unique. Consequently their distinctions remain valid after passing to costs modulo constants.

### A conjugate-only example

Take

\[
a=(-1-i,-1+i,-2+i,2),\qquad
b=(2+i,-1+i,0,i).
\]

The real part of the product vector is

\[
c=(10,0,7,4,-2,10,24,4,-8,16,-5,-4,-4,14),\qquad Q(c)=132.
\]

Its Hessian has rank twelve. One kernel direction is the actual imaginary part, with gradient scale \(+1\). The other primitive direction has scale \(+239\). Equation (21) proves that these exhaust the possible aligned directions. Therefore this cost is an actual conjugate secant but **cannot be the sum of two real invariant-cone points**. In particular it cannot be replaced by two ordinary real spectral product tests.

### A real-real-only example

Add the two ordinary real product vectors arising from

\[
a^{(0)}=(-3,0,3,2),\quad b^{(0)}=(2,-1,1,-4),
\]

\[
a^{(1)}=(0,3,-2,1),\quad b^{(1)}=(4,1,-2,-4).
\]

Their sum has coordinates

\[
c=(184,93,16,-47,20,148,66,34,-14,242,-26,172,48,-112),
\quad Q(c)=-51840.
\]

The Hessian again has rank twelve. The two aligned directions have gradient scales

\[
-1\quad\text{and}\quad-337982862721.
\]

Thus (18) has no solution: this sum of two real spectral tests is **not** one actual complex spectral support cost, even after a constant shift. It is not a counterexample to any support inequality.

### An overlap example

Take

\[
a=(-i,2+i,-2+i,-1+i),\qquad b=(-1-i,2,-i,0).
\]

Then

\[
c=(-22,-13,-2,2,-5,2,3,6,12,15,-2,-2,-18,-1),\quad Q(c)=144.
\]

The two scales are \(+1\) and \(-1\). In particular the negative direction can be chosen as

\[
d'=(18,7,6,10,15,2,3,6,12,15,2,2,18,-1).
\]

Both \((c+d')/2\) and \((c-d')/2\) are real singular points of the cubic. The same cost therefore has a conjugate representation and a split representation. “Elliptic” and “split” descriptions of a chosen eight-point lift cannot be treated as disjoint classes of costs.

## 7. Exact exclusion of the common-minor facet normals

For a permutation \(\pi\), let

\[
f_\pi=
\mathbf1_{\pi(3)=3}+\mathbf1_{\pi(2)=4}
+\mathbf1_{\pi(\{1,2\})=\{1,3\}}
-\mathbf1_{\pi(\{1,3\})=\{2,3\}}.
\tag{22}
\]

This is the value vector of the representative nontrivial common-minor facet. Exact substitution gives

\[
F(f)=-1,\qquad Q(f)=-1.
\]

Hence its unique possible cubic lift is \(f-e\). But

\[
\boxed{\det H_F(f-e)=-4\ne0.}\tag{23}
\]

Its gradient is nonzero. Equation (18) therefore cannot hold: the Hessian forces \(d=0\), which would require the gradient to vanish. Equivalently, the unique lift is outside the Hessian secant divisor. For a nonzero scaling \(\lambda f\), the only shift is \(-\lambda e\), and the Hessian is \(\lambda H_F(f-e)\), still invertible.

Left and right label permutations preserve the invariant cone and fix \(e\). Thus every one of the 48 symmetry copies of this facet normal has the same exclusion. This is a restriction on actual spectral costs, not a proof of scalar MOC.

### A rational neighborhood is also excluded

Center costs by subtracting their first selected permutation value. Let \(c_0=f-e\) in the coordinates (5), and let \(u_0=0\), \(\|u\|_\infty\le\varepsilon\) on the thirteen remaining coordinates. Put \(c=c_0+u\), \(H_0=H_F(c_0)\), and

\[
\mathcal M(c)=-H_0^{-1}\{Q(c)H_F(c)-F(c)H_F(e)\}.
\]

Then \(\mathcal M(c_0)=I\). Exact coefficient bounds give

\[
\|\mathcal M(c)-I\|_\infty
\le\frac{175}{2}\varepsilon+rac{879}{2}\varepsilon^2
+\frac{5301}{2}\varepsilon^3.
\tag{24}
\]

At \(\varepsilon=1/100\), the right side is
\(1843201/2000000<1\). Also
\(|Q(c)+1|\le543/10000<1\). Thus the unique cubic lift has an invertible Hessian throughout this closed box, so the entire box is outside the projected secant and outside the actual spectral cost image.

This is a quantitative exclusion in specified quotient coordinates. Its radius is not directly comparable with a profile-space facet protrusion: converting that exclusion into a universal support estimate requires additional convex geometry.

## 8. The precise Lorentz information and its limits

For a spinor \(p=(u,v)\), define the real vector

\[
q(p)=(|u|^2+|v|^2,\,2\Re(u\bar v),\,2\Im(u\bar v),\,|u|^2-|v|^2).
\]

With the Lorentz form of signature \((1,3)\),

\[
\langle q(p),q(p)\rangle_L=0,\quad q_0(p)\ge0,
\qquad \langle q(p),q(r)\rangle_L=2|[p,r]|^2.
\tag{25}
\]

These are future-null vectors, or equivalently rank-one Hermitian positive matrices. The labelwise conjugate Segre construction therefore has a definite real positivity meaning.

For the first four points let

\[
\omega_A=\Im\{[12][34]\overline{[13][24]}\},
\]

and define \(\omega_B\) using labels \(5,6,7,8\). Direct four-spinor algebra gives

\[
\det(q_1,q_2,q_3,q_4)=-8\omega_A.
\]

Since the complex singlet block is an outer product,

\[
\boxed{Q(\Re(\eta m))=-|\eta|^2\omega_A\omega_B
=-\frac{|\eta|^2}{64}\det(q_1,\ldots,q_4)\det(q_5,\ldots,q_8).}
\tag{26}
\]

In particular this determinant is independent of the phase of \(\eta\). Both signs occur, so there is no universal positive or negative singlet-determinant condition for MOC costs. For four distinct points, its vanishing in one family says that the corresponding cross ratio is real, equivalently that those four points lie on a generalized circle. Degenerate configurations are retained by the polynomial identity.

There is also a useful positive moment inequality. Let
\(G_{ij}=|[p_i,p_j]|^2\), \(t_i=\|p_i\|^2\), and let \(S\) have the three spatial coordinates of \(q(p_i)\) as rows. Then

\[
G=\frac12(tt^T-SS^T),\qquad \operatorname{rank}G\le4.
\tag{27}
\]

For a real matrix \(W\succeq0\) that is entrywise nonnegative,

\[
\boxed{0\le\langle W,G\rangle\le\tfrac12 t^TWt.}\tag{28}
\]

The lower inequality is entrywise positivity; the upper one follows from
\(\operatorname{tr}(S^TWS)\ge0\). With unit spinors, \(G\) is conditionally negative semidefinite on \(\mathbf1^\perp\). This applies to the positive \(W\)-block in the dual track's moment lift. It is a valid positive budget, but no conversion of the full signed spectral cost into this budget has been established.

## 9. A concrete obstruction to a naive positive Gauss-map construction

For \(z=m(p)\), let \(c=\Re z\). Differentiating (6), using the quadratic singularity relations for \(z\) and \(\bar z\), gives

\[
\boxed{4\nabla F(c)=\sum_M\epsilon(M)|m_M(p)|^2\,\ell_M.}\tag{29}
\]

Here the right side uses nonnegative intensities
\(|m_M|^2=\prod_{\{i,j\}\in M}G_{ij}\), constrained by the future-null Gram matrix (27).

It is tempting to drop those Gram constraints and use only nonnegativity. That loses all order: the signed matching vectors satisfy

\[
\sum_M\epsilon(M)\ell_M=0,
\tag{30}
\]

because the first-power matching sum is the identically zero Pfaffian of a rank-two bracket matrix. They span all fourteen coordinates. Thus their nonnegative conic hull is the entire fourteen-dimensional vector space: each generator's negative is the sum of the others, and the generators span. In particular no nonzero linear functional is nonnegative on every signed matching generator. Formula (29) is useful only if its coupled Lorentz/Plücker constraints are retained.

There is a second exact consequence. If \(z=c+id\) is singular, then

\[
\nabla F(c)=\nabla F(d),\qquad H_F(c)d=0,
\]

and therefore

\[
\boxed{\nabla F(c\cos\theta+d\sin\theta)=\nabla F(c).}\tag{31}
\]

The phase circle of one spectral test lies in a single affine Gauss-map fiber. By contrast, the analogous split pair with opposite gradients gives a hyperbolic parametrization. This is a precise geometric distinction between the two parametrizations; the overlap example shows why it is not a disjoint classification of costs.

## 10. Higher-dimensional skew Pfaffians and their jets

This construction gives actual relations in higher-dimensional spectral coordinate spaces; it is independent of a desired positive decomposition.

For an odd positive integer \(p\) and matchings of \(2n\) labels, put

\[
P_{n,p}(x)=\sum_M\epsilon(M)\ell_M(x)^p.
\tag{32}
\]

Its \(p\)-linear polarization, evaluated on configurations
\(x^{(r)}=m(p^{(r)})\), is

\[
\operatorname{Pf}\left(\prod_{r=1}^p[p_i^{(r)},p_j^{(r)}]\right)_{i,j=1}^{2n}.
\tag{33}
\]

The matrix in (33) is the alternating Gram matrix of product tensors in
\((\mathbb C^2)^{\otimes p}\), whose dimension is \(2^p\). If \(2n>2^p\), (32) is identically zero on the entire invariant coordinate space: spectral coordinate vectors span that space, so the zero polarization implies the zero polynomial.

If \(2n\le2^p\), select \(n\) distinct complementary pairs of binary strings of length \(p\), and use their product basis vectors for the \(2n\) labels. Their Gram matrix pairs only complementary strings and has Pfaffian \(\pm1\). Thus (32) is a nonzero polynomial.

At a single actual spectral configuration, the matrix is \(([p_i,p_j]^p)\). It is a Gram matrix in \(\operatorname{Sym}^p\mathbb C^2\), of dimension \(p+1\). Consequently

\[
\boxed{p+1<2n\le2^p\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
P_{n,p}\text{ is a nonzero spectral relation}.}\tag{34}
\]

More precisely, its \(k\)-th derivative at an actual configuration vanishes whenever

\[
(p-k+1)2^k<2n.\tag{35}
\]

To prove (35), polarize the remaining \(k\) directions using spectral configurations. The relevant Gram space is
\(\operatorname{Sym}^{p-k}\mathbb C^2\otimes(\mathbb C^2)^{\otimes k}\), with exactly the displayed dimension. A rank bound kills its Pfaffian. Multilinearity extends the result to arbitrary coordinate directions.

For \(n=4,p=3\), the value and first derivative vanish because the ranks are bounded by four and six, while the full polarized tensor space has dimension eight. This explains the exceptional nonzero cubic and its singularity along \(M_8\). For \(n\ge5\), the analogous cubic is identically zero on the whole coordinate space and cannot supply an induction. Nonzero higher odd powers are needed.

There is also a secant version. On a combination of two spectral points, a term with \(r\) repeated copies of one and \(p-r\) of the other has Gram dimension
\((r+1)(p-r+1)\). For odd \(p=2k-1\), the maximum is \(k(k+1)\). Hence

\[
\boxed{k(k+1)<2n\le2^{2k-1}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
P_{n,2k-1}\text{ vanishes on the entire secant variety}.}\tag{36}
\]

These are concrete tensor/exterior-algebra relations for higher-dimensional actual costs. Relations alone do not provide the positive gluing required for an all-dimensional MOC proof.

## 11. What the remaining proof must establish

For a fixed unitary profile \(R\), the unresolved task is the support inequality (2) on the **actual real branch** characterized by (18), or a valid stronger inequality on the whole real projected secant. The exact common-facet exclusion proves that certain arbitrary linear obstructions cannot be spectral. It does not exclude all normal cones producing a support violation.
