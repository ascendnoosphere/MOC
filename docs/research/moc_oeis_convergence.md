# MOC: sequence identities, exact convergence, and a global error bound

**Status.** This pass does not prove unrestricted MOC, Chollet, Rota, or Erdős #1020. It proves the identities and convergence statements below, independently checks the dimension-four polynomial relation space, audits numerical convergence, and sharpens the exact full-unitary MOC4 target. Known OEIS and representation-theoretic identities are not claimed as new discoveries.

## 2. The Catalan match has an all-dimensional proof

Let
\[
z_\pi(a,b)=\prod_{i=1}^n(a_i+b_{\pi(i)}),\qquad
\Phi_n:\mathbb C[S_n]\to\mathbb C[a,b],\quad\pi\mapsto z_\pi.
\]
Here the span is a span of **polynomials**, not of their numerical values at fixed spectra.

**Theorem.** rank Phi_n=C_n and dim ker Phi_n=n!−C_n. For n≥3, the kernel is exactly the two-sided ideal generated by a three-letter antisymmetrizer.

**Proof.** On V=(C²), let P_pi send the tensor-basis subset I to pi(I). Put
\[
x(a)=\bigotimes_i(a_i,1)^T,\qquad y(b)^T=\bigotimes_j(1,b_j).
\]
Then y(b)^T P_pi x(a)=z_pi. Both x(a) and y(b)^T span their respective tensor spaces: taking each parameter from {0,1} already provides tensor-product bases. Hence
\[
\sum c_\pi z_\pi\equiv0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\sum c_\pi P_\pi=0.
\]
Schur–Weyl duality therefore gives
\[
\operatorname{rank}\Phi_n=
\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
\left(\binom nk-\binom n{k-1}\right)^2.
\]
Writing d_k=binomial(n,k)−binomial(n,k−1), one has d_(n+1−k)=−d_k, so the sum is
\[
\frac12\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}d_k^2
=\binom{2n}{n}-\binom{2n}{n-1}=C_n.
\]
The three-letter antisymmetrizer vanishes because exterior³(C²)=0. Conversely, every symmetric-group irreducible with at least three rows contains the S_3 sign representation upon restriction: remove corner boxes down to the diagram (1,1,1). The antisymmetrizer is thus nonzero in precisely these simple blocks. A nonzero element generates its full matrix block as a two-sided ideal. These are exactly the blocks absent from the tensor representation, proving the kernel assertion. ∎

The transformed match has a concrete explanation too:
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{n}d_k^2=2C_n-1,
\]
because the omitted last term is d_(n+1)²=1. It counts redundant reflected block dimensions; it does not provide a positivity inequality.

For n=4, independent exact coefficient elimination gives rank 14 and nullity 10. More explicitly, use columns

1234, 1243, 1324, 1342, 1423, 2134, 2143, 2314, 2341, 2413, 3124, 3142, 3412, 4123

and rows (S,T)

(∅,∅), (3,3), (2,2), (3,2), (2,3), (2,1), (13,13), (3,1), (1,1), (12,13), (1,3), (13,12), (12,12), (4,2).

The integer matrix M_((S,T),pi)=1_{pi(S)=T} has determinant −1. Here 13 means {1,3}. This gives a direct rank lower bound, while the theorem gives the matching upper bound. Modular coefficient ranks for n=1,...,5 are 1,2,5,14,42; these finite checks are not substituted for the proof.

The Temperley–Lieb interpretation and the crucial distinction between the linear span and the realizable spectral-product variety already appear in `docs/theorems/finite_and_spin_geometry/categorical_exterior_order.md`. At n=4, the projective cost variety has complex dimension five, not thirteen. Passing to all fourteen independent linear costs loses substantive nonlinear constraints.

## 3. An alternating permanent sequence has a positive counting formula

For n≥2 let A_n=(nI−J)/(n−1), a correlation matrix, and define
\[
P_n=\operatorname{per}A_n,\quad Q_n=\operatorname{per}(A_n\circ A_n),
\quad p_n=(n-1)^nP_n,\quad q_n=(n-1)^{2n}Q_n.
\]
Expansion by the rows using the all-ones part proves
\[
\operatorname{per}(aI+bJ)=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk k!a^{n-k}b^k.
\]
Consequently
\[
p_n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk k!n^{n-k}(-1)^k,
\qquad
q_n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk k![n(n-2)]^{n-k}.
\tag{1}
\]
The OEIS A134095 identity is the cancellation-free formula
\[
\boxed{p_n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk(n-k)^k k^{n-k}.}
\tag{2}
\]
Here is a proof, not merely a sequence fit. Let T(x)=xe^{T(x)} be the exponential generating function of rooted labelled trees. The right side of (2) counts a labelled map together with a two-colouring such that every directed edge changes colour: choose the k vertices of one colour, then every vertex independently maps to the other colour class. Each functional-graph component has an even directed cycle and exactly two compatible colourings. Its generating function is therefore
\[
\exp\left(\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{2T(x)^{2m}}{2m}\right)
=\frac1{1-T(x)^2}.
\]
Under x=t e^(−t), formal coefficient change of variables gives
\[
[x^n]\frac1{1-T(x)^2}
=[t^n]\frac{(1-t)e^{nt}}{1-t^2}
=[t^n]\frac{e^{nt}}{1+t}
=\sum_{j=0}^n\frac{n^j(-1)^{n-j}}{j!}.
\]
Multiplication by n! agrees with (1), proving (2). The empty case uses 0^0=1.

The same numerators are diagonal evaluations of the fixed-point polynomial
\[
F_n(x)=\sum_{\pi\in S_n}x^{\mathrm{fix}(\pi)}
=n!\sum_{j=0}^n\frac{(x-1)^j}{j!}:
\quad p_n=(-1)^nF_n(1-n),\quad q_n=F_n((n-1)^2).
\]
Thus the useful transform is a rencontres-polynomial evaluation, not an unexplained rescaling of a short list.

## 4. Proven convergence, with correction terms

For the preceding family,
\[
\boxed{P_n=\frac e2\left(1+\frac1{4n}+\frac{13}{48n^2}+O(n^{-3})\right),}
\]
\[
\boxed{Q_n=1+\frac1{2n^2}+O(n^{-3}),\qquad
\frac{Q_n}{P_n^2}=\frac4{e^2}\left(1-\frac1{2n}+\frac7{48n^2}+O(n^{-3})\right).}
\tag{3}
\]

**Proof.** Termwise integration of (1) gives
\[
\frac{p_n}{n^n}=I_n=\int_0^\infty e^{-t}(1-t/n)^n\,dt.
\]
The signed tail beyond n is exactly (−1)^n e^(−n)n!/n^n, which is O(sqrt(n)e^(−2n)). On [0,n], expand
\[
e^{-t}(1-t/n)^n=e^{-2t}
\left(1-\frac{t^2}{2n}+\frac{-t^3/3+t^4/8}{n^2}\right)
+\text{remainder}.
\]
Splitting at 3 log n bounds the integrated remainder by O(n^(−3)): on the first part Taylor's formula gives an integrable polynomial times e^(−2t)/n³; on the second part the original integrand is at most e^(−2t), and the displayed polynomial tails are smaller than the required error. Since the j-th exponential moment is j!/2^(j+1),
\[
I_n=\frac12-\frac1{8n}-\frac1{32n^2}+O(n^{-3}).
\]
Multiply by (n/(n−1))^n=e(1+1/(2n)+11/(24n²)+O(n^(−3))) to obtain P_n.

For Q_n, classify permutations by their k moved points. If D_k denotes the derangement number, then
\[
Q_n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk\frac{D_k}{(n-1)^{2k}}.
\]
The k=0 term is 1, the k=1 term vanishes, and the k=2 term is n/[2(n−1)³]. Using D_k≤k!, the remaining terms are bounded by the geometric tail with ratio n/(n−1)², hence O(n^(−3)). This proves Q_n and then the ratio expansion. ∎

At n=128 the exact-integer computation gives P≈1.3618181543, Q≈1.0000314083 and Q/P²≈0.5392316827. The rescaled second-order coefficients are approximately 0.2732553 and 0.1562780, approaching the proven 13/48 and 7/48. The proof, not those decimal samples, justifies the limits.

## 5. Roots-of-unity filtering explains another observed sequence

Take two pole spinors e_1,e_2 and m equatorial spinors
\[
(e_1+\omega^j e_2)/\sqrt2,\qquad0\le j<m,\quad\omega=e^{2\pi i/m}.
\]
Let B_(m+2) be their Gram matrix and write P~,Q~ for per(B) and per(B∘conjugate(B)). Fischer norm has the convention ||x^alpha||²=alpha!, so
\[
\prod_iL_i(x,y)=2^{-m/2}xy\{x^m-(-y)^m\},\qquad
\boxed{\widetilde P_{m+2}=2(m+1)!/2^m.}
\tag{4}
\]
For the entrywise absolute square the product polynomial is
\[
2^{-m}ad\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}(a+d+\omega^{-j}b+\omega^jc).
\]
Put c_(m,k)=m binomial(m−k,k)/(m−k). The cyclic product is
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}(-1)^k c_{m,k}s^{m-2k}(bc)^k
+(-1)^{m-1}(b^m+c^m),\qquad s=a+d.
\tag{5}
\]
For a direct derivation, factor c z²+s z+b=c(z−alpha)(z−beta) and take the product over z^m=1, dividing by the product of these roots. The roots alpha,beta have sum −s/c and product b/c. Their power sums obey the second-order recurrence, giving the central Dickson polynomial D_0=2, D_1=s, D_m=sD_(m−1)−bcD_(m−2), and the two remaining terms in (5). Polynomial continuation includes c=0. Exact resultant checks verify (5) independently for m=1,...,5.

Orthogonality of different b,c exponents and
\[
\|ad(a+d)^\ell\|_F^2=\ell!2^{\ell-2}(\ell^2+3\ell+4)
\]
give
\[
\boxed{h_m:=4^m\widetilde Q_{m+2}
=2m!+\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}
 c_{m,k}^2(k!)^2(m-2k)!2^{m-2k-2}
 ((m-2k)^2+3(m-2k)+4).}
\tag{6}
\]
The integer sequence begins
\[
6,36,312,3584,49480,789408,14228592,285422592.
\]
For total sizes n=5,6,7 this reproduces both the permanent and the absolute-square permanent of the saved numerical candidates. Matching two invariants does not identify their frames up to equivalence. The n=8 candidate does not match this family: its permanent is about 147.533 rather than 157.5.

There is a rigorous asymptotic:
\[
\boxed{\widetilde Q_{m+2}\sim\frac{m!m^2}{3\,2^m},\qquad
\frac{\widetilde Q_{m+2}}{\widetilde P_{m+2}^2}
\sim\frac{2^m}{12m!}\to0.}
\tag{7}
\]
To prove it, divide the k-th summand of (6) by the k=0 summand. The ratio is
\[
4^{-k}\frac{m^2(m-k-1)!^2}{(m-2k)!m!}
\frac{(m-2k)^2+3(m-2k)+4}{m^2+3m+4}.
\]
For fixed k this tends to 4^(−k). The middle factorial factor starts at 1, has next value m/(m−1), and thereafter has successive ratios
(m−2k)(m−2k−1)/(m−k−1)²≤1. It is at most 2 for m≥2. Dominated convergence thus gives the geometric sum 4/3; the extra 2m! is negligible. Equation (7) follows.

These calculations explain a numerical pattern. They are not evidence that the dimension-wise supremum of the Chollet ratio follows either (3) or (7). Indeed any counterexample A would satisfy R(A⊕I_k)=R(A)>1 in every higher dimension, independently of the limits of these two families.

## 6. Local optimizer convergence is not a global bound

The audit excludes the small saved run because it overlaps the large run. There are 3,536 distinct saved fixed-spectrum optimization records. The 2,160 dimension-four records in that run used **real orthogonal** frames, not general complex unitaries.

| Old n=4 direction | Runs | Small gradient but support gap below −10^(−6) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Random direction | 720 | 231 |
| First selected hull facet | 720 | 190 |
| Second selected hull facet | 720 | 170 |

The support gap means Re(eta det)−max_pi Re(eta z_pi), divided by the recorded scale. A negative value here means the optimizer did not even attain the known best permutation; it is not evidence for a positive MOC margin over the whole orbit.

Nine new traces in `build/direct_attack/moc_convergence_traces.json` use complex Givens parameters covering the unitary orbit, three spectral configurations, and three starts each. They store every iteration, both chart and intrinsic gradient norms, and final matrices. An independent finite-difference gradient check had maximum error 1.74e−10.

Four runs declared optimizer success at suboptimal points. Explicit pi/4 row rotations increased their normalized objectives by approximately
\[
0.1174015016,\quad0.0613232811,\quad0.00002113549,\quad0.0678529097.
\]
These are finite increases at the saved numerical matrices, not an inference from small gradients.

The predicted values follow from an exact identity at a monomial frame P. If G_ij(theta) rotates two rows, multilinearity or the 2×2 determinant expansion gives
\[
\det(D_a+G_{ij}(\theta)PD_bP^*G_{ij}(\theta)^*)
=\cos^2\theta\,z_\pi+\sin^2\theta\,z_{\pi'},
\tag{8}
\]
where pi' swaps those two assignments. A positive transposition gain therefore gives a positive second derivative and a pi/4 increase of half that gain. The observed increases agree with those predictions. The phase-angle charts also lose differential rank at monomial frames. None of these local numerical procedures is a certified global maximization method.

## 7. A uniform numerical error theorem directly for full MOC

Unlike a family-specific limit, the following bound controls **all spectra and all unitaries** in a fixed dimension.

Normalize max_i|a_i|,max_j|b_j|≤1 and define
\[
D(a,b,U)=\det(D_a+UD_bU^*),\quad
K(a,b)=\operatorname{conv}\{z_\pi(a,b)\},\quad
F(a,b,U)=\operatorname{dist}(D(a,b,U),K(a,b)).
\]
MOC in dimension n is precisely F=0 throughout this compact domain. A general spectral pair reduces to this normalization by common scaling; the determinant and hull both scale by the same scalar power.

**Theorem.** With operator norm on U and supremum norm on the spectra,
\[
\boxed{|F(a,b,U)-F(a',b',U')|
\le n2^n\{\|a-a'\|_\infty+\|b-b'\|_\infty+\|U-U'\|_{op}\}.}
\tag{9}
\]

**Proof.** Write da,db,dU for the three differences. Both matrices inside the determinant have norm at most 2, and their difference has norm at most da+db+2dU. Telescoping the determinant one column at a time and using Hadamard's inequality gives
\[
|D-D'|\le n2^{n-1}(da+db+2dU).
\]
The same telescoping bound on the n scalar factors gives
\[
\max_\pi|z_\pi-z'_\pi|\le n2^{n-1}(da+db).
\]
Pairing identical convex weights bounds the Hausdorff distance between K and K' by this maximum. Distance to a compact set is 1-Lipschitz in its point argument and in Hausdorff perturbations of the set. Adding the two bounds proves (9). ∎

**Certified-cover consequence.** Suppose a finite sample is an epsilon-net in the maximum of those three metrics, and F at every sample is at most a certified tau. Then
\[
\boxed{\sup F\le\tau+3n2^n\epsilon.}
\tag{10}
\]
For n=4 the covering error is **192 epsilon**. If sample evaluations carry an additional absolute error sigma, add sigma to the right side. No such net or interval certification is claimed for the local searches above.

There is an elementary finite construction of nets in principle. For epsilon≤1, grid each real and imaginary matrix entry on [−1,1] with spacing at most epsilon/(4n), retain grid matrices with smallest singular value at least 1/2, and take their unitary polar factors. A grid approximation X to U has ||X−U||_op≤sqrt(2)n times the spacing. Its polar factor V obeys ||V−U||_op≤2||X−U||_op: the singular values of X differ from 1 by at most ||X−U||, so ||X−V|| has that same bound. Thus the retained polar factors cover U(n). Spectral grids, projected onto the unit disk, cover the spectral coordinates. This is a very expensive construction, not a computation performed in this pass.

For exact sample evaluations on nets with epsilon tending to zero, the maximum sampled F and its covering-error upper bound converge to the true dimension-wise supremum. For numerical certification, evaluation uncertainty must tend to zero too. More precisely, if the sample enclosures at level k are [ell_(k,j),u_(k,j)], then
\[
\max_j\ell_{k,j}\le\sup F\le
\max_j u_{k,j}+3n2^n\epsilon_k.
\]
Both endpoints converge if epsilon_k tends to zero and max_j(u_(k,j)−ell_(k,j)) tends to zero. Cumulative maxima of the lower bounds and cumulative minima of the upper bounds make the bounds monotone, but do not remove fixed evaluation uncertainty or persistent slack. This is the uniform convergence actually needed; a decreasing sequence of local optimization endpoints does not provide it.

## 8. The full MOC4 target after this pass

`docs/theorems/profile_geometry/analysis_full_cayley_quartic.md` proves that the unrestricted unitary problem is equivalent to nonnegativity of an actual spectral quartic
\[
A+Bt+Ct^2+Dt^3+Et^4,
\]
with A,E≥0 and an explicit one-parameter Gram matrix. Its new Section 4.2 eliminates the Gram parameter for A,E>0. Put
\[
\beta=B/(A^{3/4}E^{1/4}),\quad
\delta=D/(A^{1/4}E^{3/4}),\quad
s=|\beta+\delta|/2,\quad q=|\beta-\delta|/2.
\]
The sharp remaining inequality is
\[
\boxed{C\ge\sqrt{AE}\max_{r\ge2}
\{2-r^2+sr+q\sqrt{r^2-4}\}.}
\tag{11}
\]
The maximum has a unique explicitly characterized algebraic maximizer when q>0; the q=0 formula is piecewise quadratic. At the threshold there is an explicit factorization at a root of multiplicity at least two, hence a constructive SOS above it. These statements are proved for arbitrary quartics in the linked note.

**What is not proved:** that the linked spectral coefficients satisfy (11) for every nondegenerate actual input, with the degenerate inputs handled by the original Gram condition. The Catalan count, the positive functional-graph formula, the two family limits, and the local traces do not imply that assertion. It is the same full MOC4 gap, now stated as an explicit scalar inequality rather than an unperformed optimization.

For Rota, the catalog's (n!)^n counts all candidate row arrangements, not successful arrangements. For Erdős #1020, binomial(n,3)−binomial(n−2,3)=(n−2)² counts one endpoint construction, not an extremal upper bound. No full solution of either problem follows from those sequences.
