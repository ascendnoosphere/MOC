Dense Haar analysis of the 20,127-record MOC campaign

The strongest result from this analysis is a concrete simplification of the
high-dimensional numerical witnesses. Every one of the 1,298 dense Haar cases
with n >= 32 has a three-product containment witness drawn from its first 128
uniformly sampled permutations. The original spectral-order heuristics are not
needed for those witnesses. This concerns these sampled matrices and does not
establish MOC for arbitrary matrices or dimensions.

The analysis reads the existing combined file without changing it. Its SHA256
is 079d7550cedeb1b6743404140b1c94855e1065690ae214e9da0cf639e36e0ff0.
The underlying dense campaign contains 1,578 matrices spanning every integer
n=4,...,1000. Spectral families are Gaussian, near-real, clustered, log-radius,
and perturbed-circle cancellation. Their parameters are specified in
code/research/moc_campaign_dense.py. Each matrix is a fully dense Haar orbit
sample, unlike the fixed-rank reflector campaign.

What was reconstructed

For n >= 32, the RNG state was replayed through the original spectra and matrix
draw, then the original permutation products were regenerated. QR does not
consume random numbers, so most replays did not require a new factorization.
Every reconstructed maximum log product agrees exactly with its stored value.
The analysis compares the original mixed list, consisting of 256 random
permutations and at most 26 sorted-feature permutations, with the first 128
random permutations alone. The second list has no spectral-order enrichment.

Forty checkpoint matrices at n=32,64,128,256,512,1000 were also reconstructed
with QR to evaluate row and column Hadamard determinant bounds. Smaller
checkpoint dimensions have two examples per family; larger dimensions have
one. These forty cases were selected by dimension, before testing the bounds.

All numerical witnesses remain floating-point results. Their exact geometric
implications are proved below, but the supplied random matrices and products
have not been enclosed with interval arithmetic.

Heldout dimensional predictions

Let q_n = (log|det(A+B)| - log(max sampled |z_pi|))/n. The maximum here is over
the original mixed permutation list, not the complete n! set. Four candidate
models were considered: c, c+a/n, c+a/sqrt(n), and c+a log(n)/n. Selection used
training dimensions 128...255 and validation dimensions 256...511. The selected
model was refit on 128...511 and evaluated once on 512...1000. A split using
n modulo 5 would be invalid here because family selection in the dense sweep
itself uses n modulo 5.

The Gaussian fit is c=-0.610438; its heldout mean error is +0.004740. The
log-radius fit is c=-0.569586 with mean error -0.001462. The cancellation fit
is -0.705198 + 0.333852/sqrt(n), and the near-real fit is
-0.691504 + 3.477541/n. These are finite-range predictors. In particular, the
cancellation intercept should not be promoted to a limiting constant. The
cluster fit has heldout mean error +0.019620, substantially worse than the
other families. Its apparent rate continues to drift across the split.

Source-separated fits for all six reflector families are saved alongside
these results. For Gaussian spectra, the reflector heldout mean is -0.455573
and the dense Haar mean is -0.615179. These campaigns use different permutation
lists and different mixing ensembles, so their difference is descriptive,
not an isolated causal effect of mixer rank. Other nominally similar family
names also use different parameters, notably the near-real perturbation.

Angles and radii explain different parts of containment

For each product list, the largest cyclic gap between product arguments
measures angular coverage. At n >= 512, the first-128-random list has median
gap 0.258518 radians and maximum 0.526925 radians, far below pi. A reference
simulation of 10,000 lists of 128 independent uniform angles has median gap
0.256524 and 95th percentile 0.375968. The permutation products are dependent,
so this reference is a comparison, not a justified probabilistic model.

The high-dimensional family-specific median gaps are 0.251179 for Gaussian,
0.265205 near-real, 0.260058 clustered, 0.252036 log-radius, and 0.264789
cancellation. Their two-sample Kolmogorov distances from the reference gap
distribution range from 0.0432 to 0.1371; near-real has the largest distance.
The observed angular coverage is therefore similar in scale across families,
while the radial behavior differs sharply.

For example, at n >= 512 the median, across cases, of the median random-product
log modulus minus the determinant log modulus is approximately +134.68 for
Gaussian and +132.70 for clusters, but -21.21 for near-real. A typical sampled
near-real product is smaller than the determinant. Its successful witnesses
use the upper tail of product magnitudes in several directions. The worst
high-dimensional near-real three-sector disk witness still has logarithmic
radius slack 3.55015, a factor of about 34.8 over |det|. Thus a blanket claim
that the determinant is smaller than a typical random product would be false.

Sparse witnesses and the failures of a rigid sector rule

A deterministic three-sector rule places centers 120 degrees apart, permits
each chosen angle to lie within 30 degrees of its center, and selects the
largest product in each sector. It tries 24 fixed rotations. The analogous
four-sector rule uses centers 90 degrees apart and half-width 22.5 degrees.
The actual angular gaps and minimum selected radius determine the disk bound.

List and dimension        Cases   3-sector successes  4-sector successes
Mixed, n >= 32            1298    1298                1298
First 128 random, n >= 32  1298    1294                1293
First 128 random, n >=128   901     900                 897
First 128 random, n >=512   501     501                 498

Adding a fourth point does not automatically improve this rule: its fourth
required sector can force a smaller minimum radius. Three points already
suffice for planar containment when chosen appropriately.

The four failures of the rotated three-sector rule occur in clustered cases
at n=34,35,43 and a near-real case at n=391. In two clustered cases no tested
rotation fills every required sector. In the other two cases the sector rule
chooses a radius too small for its angular-gap bound. These failures are not
failures of sampled-hull containment.

Optimizing the radius-angle bound over every radial threshold succeeds in all
1,298 first-128-random lists. The four failed sector cases have successful
threshold witnesses using respectively 8,14,9,11 products. Their logarithmic
disk-radius slacks are 4.85909,4.15408,8.96524,1.64019. A separate angular
construction centered at the determinant extracts a three-product triangle
in every case, including these four. Its smallest angular clearance from a
semicircle boundary is 7.93e-6 radians across all 2,596 replayed lists.
The n=391 near-real case is also the weakest optimized disk in the entire
1,298-case pure-random replay. Even there, its radius is about 5.156 times
|det|. These dense samples therefore leave a substantial untested gap to the
boundary behavior that a universal proof must control.

The optimizing algorithm sorts products by decreasing modulus, inserts their
angles into circular order, and maintains the largest gap in a heap. It checks
every radial threshold in O(m log m) time for m sampled products. Duplicate
angles retain the full circular wrap gap. Two hundred comparisons against
brute force, including tied angles, and collinear controls checked this logic.

A sufficient theorem that avoids evaluating the determinant

Let A=diag(a), B=U diag(b) U*, with U unitary. Choose permutation products
z_j=product_i(a_i+b_{pi_j(i)}), j=1,...,m. Suppose |z_j| >= r > 0, and their
arguments have maximum cyclic gap Delta < pi. Then their convex hull contains
the centered disk of radius r cos(Delta/2).

Proof: every direction has a selected argument within Delta/2. The projection
of that product onto the direction is at least r cos(Delta/2), because the
cosine is positive. The support function of the selected convex hull therefore
dominates the support function of this disk in every direction. This proves
disk containment.

Set C=(A+B)U. Its entries are C_ij=(a_i+b_j)U_ij and |det C|=|det(A+B)|.
Hadamard's inequality gives the explicitly computable bound

 H = min{ product_i sqrt(sum_j |a_i+b_j|^2 |U_ij|^2),
          product_j sqrt(sum_i |a_i+b_j|^2 |U_ij|^2) }.

Consequently H <= r cos(Delta/2) is a sufficient MOC certificate for this U.
It requires row/column norms and selected spectral products, not a determinant
evaluation. It applies to every matrix size and arbitrary complex spectra
whenever its stated inequality holds.

The optimized threshold version numerically certifies 10 of the 40 tested dense checkpoint
matrices using the enriched product list; the three-sector version certifies
7/40 and the four-sector version 1/40. No first-128-random list certifies its
checkpoint Hadamard bound. Successful enriched examples include Gaussian
n=1000, clustered n=256,512,1000, and cancellation n=512,1000. The Gaussian
n=1000 disk bound exceeds H by a factor exp(42.24). These examples show that
the sufficient theorem has computational content, while its many failures
show that Hadamard is too weak for general coverage.

An exact three-point extraction when the determinant is known

Write v_j=z_j-d, where d=det(A+B), and discard zero vectors if any occur.
If their sorted arguments have largest cyclic gap less than pi, d lies in
their convex hull. More explicitly, take the first angle alpha_0 and the two
angles immediately bracketing alpha_0+pi. Those three directions have every
cyclic gap less than pi and their positive rays surround the origin, giving
a triangle containing d. Antipodal boundary cases require separate treatment.
The numerical implementation computes each difference z_j-d using its own
shared logarithmic scale before taking its angle, avoiding the destruction
of small products by one very large product. This construction supplies the
three-permutation witnesses reported above; it is a verification algorithm,
not a bound on the unknown determinant.

Why a scalar disk argument cannot finish unrestricted MOC

Fix generic spectra and let K be their finite permutation-product polygon.
Any bound |det(A+B)| <= D that is uniform over all U must satisfy
D >= max_pi |z_pi|, because permutation matrices U realize every z_pi.
If K is a nondegenerate finite polygon, its centered inradius is strictly
less than max_pi |z_pi|. Otherwise K would both contain and be contained in
the same positive-radius disk, forcing a finite polygon to equal that disk.

Therefore a uniform scalar modulus bound followed by centered-disk containment
cannot prove MOC for these spectra, even if the scalar bound is optimal. The
same obstruction holds after translating the disk center. This is an exact
limitation of the method, not a weakness of the sampled data or Hadamard's
inequality. The successful disk tests settle interior, U-specific instances.
A complete proof needs directional information near commuting extremizers.
