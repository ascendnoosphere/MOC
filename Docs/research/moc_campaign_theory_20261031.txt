MOC CAMPAIGN: WHAT LARGE-DIMENSION DATA CAN EXPLAIN
31 October 2026

For a,b in C^n and U unitary, write
 d(U)=det(D_a+U D_b U*), z_pi=product_i(a_i+b_pi(i)).
The unrestricted conjecture asks whether d(U) belongs to conv{z_pi}.
Neither the calculations below nor a finite numerical campaign prove it.

An exact explanation for small random determinants

Suppose the 2n complex random variables a_i,b_j are mutually independent,
have mean zero, and E|a_i|^2=E|b_j|^2=1. They need not be Gaussian or
circularly symmetric. Fix an arbitrary unitary U, independent of them.
Then

 E_{a,b}|d(U)|^2 = S(U)
                 = sum_{k=0}^n sum_{|I|=|J|=k}|det U[I,J]|^4,       (1)
 n+1 <= S(U) <= 2^n.                                              (2)

Proof. The principal-minor expansion and Cauchy-Binet give

 d(U)=sum_{k,I,J} a_{I^c} b_J |det U[I,J]|^2.

Distinct monomials a_{I^c}b_J are orthogonal in complex L2: some
independent centered variable occurs on only one side of the product.
Every monomial has squared L2 norm one. This proves (1).
The compound matrix wedge^k U is unitary of size N=binom(n,k).
Its squared entry moduli form a doubly stochastic matrix. Each row's
sum of squares lies between 1/N and 1. Summing N rows gives a degree-k
contribution between 1 and N, proving (2). At a permutation U, S(U)=2^n.

If U is Haar and independent of the spectra, there is a closed formula:

 E_{U,a,b}|d(U)|^2 = (n+2)(n+3)/6.                                 (3)
 E_{a,b}|z_pi|^2 = 2^n for every fixed permutation pi.               (4)

Here is a derivation of the Haar constant which does not require MOC.
Take an n by k standard complex Gaussian matrix G=QR, with positive
diagonal in R. Q is a uniform Stiefel frame and independent of R. The
leading k by k block satisfies det(G_top)=det(Q_top)det(R).
Gaussian Gram-Schmidt gives independent |R_jj|^2 with Gamma(n-j+1,1)
laws: each residual column is isotropic Gaussian in the remaining
complex subspace. A Gamma(m,1) variable has second moment m(m+1).
Applying this both to G and to its square Gaussian top block yields

 E|det(Q_top)|^4
   = k!(k+1)!(n-k)!(n-k+1)! / (n!(n+1)!).

Multiplication by binom(n,k)^2 reduces this to
(k+1)(n-k+1)/(n+1). Summing k proves (3). Independence of the n factors
in z_pi and E|a_i+b_pi(i)|^2=2 prove (4).

The ratio of these root mean squares is

 R_n = sqrt((n+2)(n+3)/(6*2^n)).

 n       E|d|^2       R_n
 4       7            0.6614378278
 10      26           0.1593443598
 50      459.3333     6.3872526e-7
 100     1751        3.7165783e-14
 1000    167501      1.2502903e-148

These are ensemble identities, not empirical estimates. They provide a
specific mechanism by which high-dimensional Haar examples can become
easy to place inside a matching hull. They do not give the expectation
of a ratio, control the largest matching product, imply that zero lies
in the hull, or establish a typical-case statement. The numerator and
denominator can have very different tails. For example Markov only gives
P(|d| >= t*sqrt((n+2)(n+3)/6)) <= t^-2, not a comparison with a random
matching product from the same spectra.

Subtracting the empirical mean of each spectrum or normalizing its
sample variance destroys the independence used in (1). Such datasets
can test the mechanism qualitatively but must not be advertised as
literal samples for (3). Opposite scalar shifts a->a+c,b->b-c preserve
the MOC instance; independent centering of both spectra generally does
not preserve the sum.

Independent exact calculation: for the normalized four-dimensional
Fourier matrix, the degree contributions in (1) are 1,1,5/4,1,1.
Thus S(F4)=21/4, below the Haar mean 7 and permutation value 16.
code/research/verify_moc_campaign_moments.py exhausts all 256 independent
Rademacher spectra for this matrix and checks the same 21/4 directly.
It also verifies the factorial simplification and finite sum exactly
for n=1,...,100. The proof above covers all n.

The fixed-spectrum Haar mean already belongs to the matching hull

For arbitrary fixed complex a,b,

 E_U d(U) = sum_{k=0}^n e_{n-k}(a)e_k(b)/binom(n,k)
          = (1/n!) sum_pi z_pi.                                   (5)

For each k, Haar symmetry and the unit norm of a compound row give
E|det U[I,J]|^2=1/binom(n,k). For the matching average, the probability
that a uniform permutation sends I onto J is also 1/binom(n,k).
This proves both equalities. The first is also the constant-term case
of the finite free convolution identity of Marcus, Spielman and
Srivastava [1, Theorem 1.2]. A mean in the hull does not force every
sample into the hull. In data, distance to this known centroid is a
more meaningful concentration diagnostic than distance to zero when
the spectra have substantial means.

Fixed-rank phase mixing is a different regime

If rank(U-I)<=r, put D=diag(a_i+b_i) and E=U D_b U*-D_b. Then
 rank(E)<=2r, because E=(U-I)D_b U*+D_b(U*-I).
If delta=min_i|a_i+b_i|>0 and B=max_i|b_i|, then

 |d(U)/z_id| <= (1+2B/delta)^(2r).                                 (6)

Indeed ||E||<=2B and det(D+E)/det(D)=det(I+D^-1 E). At most 2r
eigenvalues of D^-1 E are nonzero; each has modulus at most 2B/delta.
If eta=||D^-1 E||<1, the corresponding lower bound is (1-eta)^(2r).
For bounded B/delta and fixed r, the difference in log magnitudes is
bounded independently of n. A rank-r phase update can be globally
dense while retaining this restriction. In near-cancellation spectra,
delta can be tiny and (6) becomes weak; those rows deserve separate
analysis. A large-n fixed-rank campaign must not be pooled with Haar
data under a single label such as random mixing.

For a single random Householder reflection the comparison is exact.
Let U=I-2vv*, where v is uniform on the complex unit sphere and
independent of the centered variance-one spectra in (1). Then

 E|d(U)|^2 = 2^n*(28n-4)/((n+1)(n+2)(n+3)).                        (7)

This formula was derived by the campaign's root researcher and checked
independently here. Put p_i=|v_i|^2; p has Dirichlet(1,...,1) law.
A principal I-minor is 1-2*sum_{i in I}p_i. A nonprincipal minor
vanishes unless its row and column sets differ by one exchange; for
that exchange i,j its squared modulus is 4p_i p_j. Averaging the
principal fourth powers over all subsets I amounts to averaging
(sum_i epsilon_i p_i)^4 over independent signs. This sign average is
sum_i p_i^4 + 6 sum_{i<j}p_i^2p_j^2. With
D=n(n+1)(n+2)(n+3), the Dirichlet expectations are 24/D and 4/D,
respectively. The diagonal contribution to E S(U) is consequently
2^n*12/((n+2)(n+3)). The total number of ordered one-exchange minors is
sum_k binom(n,k)k(n-k)=n(n-1)2^(n-2), and each has fourth moment 64/D.
Adding both contributions proves (7); n=1 follows directly.

The RMS ratio to a matching product is therefore asymptotic to
sqrt(28)/n, compared with Haar's exponential suppression. At n=2,
E S(U)=52/15; at n=4 it is 288/35. Formula (7) covers one reflection,
not a product of several reflections. Drury [5] proved MOC for the
Householder class in all dimensions, so this ensemble is a calibration
and sampling-bias control, not fresh evidence for an unresolved class.
The exact verifier also checks every minor of the rational reflection
with v=(1,2,2,4)/5, integrates the complete n=2 expression directly to
52/15, and checks the combinatorial summation for n=2,...,100. All pass.

Separate angular coverage from radial contraction

There is a precise circle-spectrum control for a measured radial
exponent near -log(2). For arbitrary unitary matrices A,B,

 (1/2pi) integral_0^{2pi} log|det(A+exp(it)B)| dt = 0.              (8)

Indeed A*B is unitary, with eigenvalues lambda_j on the circle, so the
integrand is sum_j log|1+exp(it)lambda_j|. Each term has zero integral;
the isolated logarithmic singularities are integrable. This is a
one-variable calculation and makes no independence assumption on U.

Now take both spectra to be all n-th roots of unity, and rotate the
second spectrum by exp(it). For n>=2, some cyclic matching aligns every
pair with the same phase discrepancy delta satisfying |delta|<=pi/n.
Consequently, for every t,

 max_pi |z_pi(t)| >= [2*cos(pi/(2n))]^n.

Combining with (8) proves

 average_t [(log|d(t)|-log max_pi|z_pi(t)|)/n]
   <= -log(2)-log(cos(pi/(2n))) = -log(2)+O(n^-2).                 (9)

This explains an exponent near -0.693 in the circular control without
assuming random determinants or independent matching products. The
exact-circle products are collinear: each factor a_i+b_j has a common
half-sum phase times a real cosine, and the total half-sum phase is
permutation independent. Thus this is a radial calculation, not a
two-dimensional hull argument. Its application to perturbed circles
is an empirical interpretation, since small singular values prevent a
uniform perturbation bound for log determinants.

For any finite nonzero subset of matching products, zero is strictly
inside its convex hull exactly when its maximum cyclic angular gap is
strictly smaller than pi. Thus phase spread and determinant shrinkage
are separate measurable conditions. Once zero is inside a polygon,
its origin-centered inradius is

 rho=min_edges Im(conj(z_i)*z_{i+1})/|z_{i+1}-z_i|,

with counterclockwise hull vertices. The sufficient condition |d|<rho
combines both effects. Record maximum angular gap, origin-in-hull,
log|d|-log rho, and the exact nearest-edge distance. Small |d| alone is
uninformative when the sampled products lie in a narrow angular sector.

Isotropic spectra make the joint matching set rotation invariant; they
do not make its n! products independent. All products reuse the same
2n spectral entries. Probability formulae for hulls of independent
uniform directions therefore cannot be applied to this matching set.
Near-real spectra, tight clusters, and near-cancellation examples are
useful controls against the broad angular spread of generic spectra.

An inexpensive route from a numerical inclusion to a rigorous one

Three matching products suffice to certify an individual planar
inclusion, even when n! is enormous. Use a computed hull triangulation
to locate a triangle containing d. Save its three permutations and
verify the following quantities with outward-rounded interval arithmetic:

 T=orient(z1,z2,z3)>0,
 orient(z1,z2,d)>0, orient(z2,z3,d)>0, orient(z3,z1,d)>0,
 orient(x,y,z)=Im(conj(y-x)*(z-x)).

Strict positive interval lower bounds prove membership in the full
matching hull. Failure to certify remains inconclusive. If every
available witness has d on an edge, an exact segment representation
can handle the equality. Strict containment in a three-vertex triangle
need not exist even for an interior polygon point: the center of a
square is a counterexample. A four-vertex enclosure can avoid that
degeneracy when a robust interior certificate is wanted.
This avoids verifying every hull vertex or enumerating all permutations.

For explicit complex-disc errors |z_i-zhat_i|<=eps_i and
|d-dhat|<=eps_d, an edge orientation error is at most

 |zhat_j-zhat_i|*(eps_d+eps_i)
 + |dhat-zhat_i|*(eps_i+eps_j)
 + (eps_i+eps_j)*(eps_d+eps_i).

This follows from expanding the bilinear orientation. A floating-point
orientation larger than this certified error is positive for the exact
inputs. Product logs and a shared positive scale help with overflow,
but normalization and exponentiation errors still require bounds.

The intended U must be exactly unitary. An arbitrary computed QR array
is only approximately unitary. Convenient exact inputs are products of
rational complex Givens matrices or U=(I+K)(I-K)^-1 for rational
skew-Hermitian K. For an existing Haar sample one can instead define
the intended U as the polar factor and bound the correction, then
propagate determinant error. For nonsingular M, if eta=||M^-1 Delta||<1,

 |det(M+Delta)-det(M)| <= |det M|*((1+eta)^n-1).

Use a verified inverse norm/residual and interval LU to make this an
actual certificate. Increasing arbitrary precision until digits agree
is useful debugging but does not itself supply an error enclosure.

Literature and its limits

[1] Adam W. Marcus, Daniel A. Spielman, Nikhil Srivastava,
    Finite free convolutions of polynomials, arXiv:1504.00350v2.
    https://arxiv.org/pdf/1504.00350v2
    Read Theorem 1.2: expected characteristic polynomials for arbitrary
    complex normal matrices. Its real-rootedness conclusions require
    real-rooted inputs and do not establish unrestricted complex MOC.

[2] Anirban Basak and Amir Dembo, Limiting Spectral Distribution of
    Sums of Unitary and Orthogonal Matrices, arXiv:1208.5100v4.
    https://arxiv.org/pdf/1208.5100v4
    Read Theorem 1.2 and the introduction's logarithmic-potential setup.
    Their ensemble is a sum of independent Haar unitary/orthogonal
    matrices. It is not the general Gaussian-eigenvalue normal ensemble
    of this campaign. Weak empirical spectral convergence alone does
    not control log determinants because log is singular at zero.
    Small singular values require separate analysis. Free-probability
    heuristics must not be silently promoted to a determinant theorem.

[3] Elizabeth Meckes, The Random Matrix Theory of the Classical Compact
    Groups, Section 1.2, Gaussian Gram-Schmidt construction, pp.22-23.
    https://case.edu/artsci/math/esmeckes/Haar_book.pdf
    Checked the complex QR construction and its phase-normalization
    warning. The fourth-minor calculation above is supplied explicitly.

[4] Alexander Kovacec, A conjecture more precise and stronger than the
    one by Marcus and de Oliveira, DMUC 26-11, 7 April 2026.
    https://www.mat.uc.pt/preprints/ps/p2611.pdf
    Primary-source status evidence that MOC is still treated as a
    conjecture in this 2026 work. Its stronger polygon assertion is
    distinct from MOC; the repository has a local counterexample to
    that stronger assertion. This literature check found no source
    proving unrestricted n=4. It is not an exhaustive novelty search.

[5] S. W. Drury, OMC for Householder reflections, Linear Algebra and
    its Applications 298 (1999), 159-169.
    https://doi.org/10.1016/S0024-3795(99)00173-1
